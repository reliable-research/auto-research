## N1/N2 Motivation (verbatim from corpus record `proposal.motivation`)
The rapid adoption of large language models has created an urgent need to measure how much AI-generated or AI-modified text is present in real-world information ecosystems. Existing approaches overwhelmingly focus on instance-level detection, classifying individual documents or sentences as human- or AI-generated. However, empirical and theoretical evidence shows that this framing is fundamentally unstable: detectors degrade under paraphrasing and adversarial pressure and are bounded by shrinking distributional differences between human and AI text, as demonstrated by @sadasivan2023detectability and @mitchell2023detectgpt.

Beyond instability, instance-level detectors introduce systematic harms. Training-based classifiers exhibit bias against non-native English writers and other linguistic minorities, as shown by @liang2023bias, making document-level labeling problematic for high-stakes social contexts. At the same time, several studies suggest that AI effects manifest more reliably in aggregate than at the level of individual texts. @tulchinskii2023intrinsic shows that global statistical properties separate human and AI text more consistently than per-sample judgments, while @bommasani2022monoculture demonstrates that algorithmic influence can be invisible in individual outcomes yet detectable at the population level.

This gap creates an opportunity to reconceptualize AI text detection as a measurement problem rather than a classification problem. Instead of asking which documents are AI-generated, we ask what fraction of a corpus reflects substantial AI modification. This shift enables stable estimation, avoids individual-level bias, and aligns with the types of questions policymakers, platform designers, and social scientists need to answer when assessing the systemic impact of LLMs.

## N6 Methodology (verbatim from corpus record `proposal.method`)
We propose a distributional maximum-likelihood framework that estimates the proportion of AI-modified text in a corpus by modeling it as a mixture of human-written and AI-generated reference distributions. The core idea is to exploit stable differences in aggregate token usage between human and AI text, avoiding instance-level decisions while retaining statistical rigor.

1. **Reference Corpus Construction**: We construct two reference corpora: one consisting of text known to be human-written and another generated by prompting LLMs with the same instructions given to human authors. This mirrors the empirical setup in @liang2023peerreview, ensuring that human and AI texts are comparable in task and context.

2. **Token Occurrence Distribution Estimation**: From each reference corpus, we estimate token occurrence probabilities over a restricted, empirically stable vocabulary (e.g., adjectives). This choice is motivated by observed linguistic differences in peer reviews reported by @liang2023peerreview and by the need for low-variance global statistics emphasized in @tulchinskii2023intrinsic.

3. **Mixture Likelihood Formulation**: We model the target corpus as samples drawn from a mixture of the human and AI distributions, parameterized by a single mixing coefficient representing the fraction of AI-modified text. This reframing directly addresses the instability of instance-level detectors identified by @sadasivan2023detectability.

4. **Maximum Likelihood Estimation**: The mixing coefficient is estimated by maximizing the corpus-level log-likelihood under the mixture model. This yields a statistically efficient estimator that aggregates weak distributional signals rather than amplifying them through classification thresholds, contrasting with approaches like @mitchell2023detectgpt.

5. **Validation via Semi-Synthetic Mixtures**: We validate the estimator by constructing synthetic corpora with known AI proportions, ensuring robustness across domains and prompts. This population-level validation aligns with the aggregate analysis perspective advocated by @bommasani2022monoculture.

Together, these components form a coherent system: reference distributions capture stable human–AI differences, the mixture model formalizes AI usage as a population parameter, and MLE provides an efficient and unbiased estimator. By design, the system avoids the fundamental and social limitations of instance-level detection while enabling scalable monitoring of AI-modified content.
