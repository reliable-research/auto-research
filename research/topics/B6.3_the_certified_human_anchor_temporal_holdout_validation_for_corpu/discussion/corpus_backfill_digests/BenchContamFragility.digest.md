## N1/N2 Motivation (verbatim from corpus record `proposal.motivation`)
Benchmark contamination detection for language models is largely built on a single operational assumption: if a model has seen benchmark samples during training, those members should remain statistically distinguishable from unseen non-members. This assumption appears in likelihood-based membership inference from @mireshghallah2022memorization, perturbation-based neighborhood comparison from @mattern2023neighbourhood, and benchmark-level output-distribution methods from @dong2024generalization. These approaches differ in implementation, but they all rely on a persistent gap in quantities such as log-probability, response confidence, or output diversity between seen and unseen examples.

That assumption becomes questionable in large reasoning models. Modern LRMs are typically produced through staged post-training pipelines in which supervised fine-tuning is followed by RL, as reflected by @guo2025deepseekr1, and their behavior is dominated by long chain-of-thought reasoning and inference-time scaling, as emphasized by @jaech2024o1. This creates two technical failure modes for existing detectors. First, contamination introduced during SFT may be altered by subsequent RL, so the statistical signatures used for membership inference may no longer track training exposure. Second, contamination with CoT on already-strong LRMs may improve confidence on both members and distributionally similar non-members, so contamination manifests as generalized reasoning competence rather than sequence-level memorization.

This gap matters because leaderboard inflation can then persist without the usual forensic evidence. If PPO-style RL can preserve benchmark gains while shrinking the member/non-member likelihood gap, and if CoT contamination on advanced LRMs boosts confidence on unseen but similar samples, then current contamination audits will systematically underestimate risk. The opportunity is therefore to re-evaluate contamination detection under the actual training regimes of LRMs and to identify which parts of the post-training pipeline, especially PPO-style importance sampling and clipping suggested by the contrast between @dong2023raft and @xiong2025raftpp, are responsible for concealment.

## N6 Methodology (verbatim from corpus record `proposal.method`)
We propose a two-stage empirical and analytical framework for studying benchmark contamination in large reasoning models. The core idea is to test contamination not as a static memorization artifact, but as a dynamic property shaped by reasoning-model post-training: SFT may introduce contamination, PPO-style RL may conceal its statistical traces, and CoT-based final-stage SFT on advanced LRMs may induce broad confidence gains that invalidate memorization-based detectors. This formulation directly targets the gap exposed by @mireshghallah2022memorization, @mattern2023neighbourhood, and @dong2024generalization under the staged LRM pipelines highlighted by @guo2025deepseekr1 and @jaech2024o1.

1. **Stage-I Contamination Pipeline**: We simulate contamination during the pre-LRM phase by introducing benchmark members in SFT and then applying clean or contaminated RL afterward. This component is needed to model the realistic pipeline where reasoning ability is acquired after initial supervised adaptation, following the staged training pattern discussed by @guo2025deepseekr1. It lets us measure whether contamination that is initially detectable remains detectable after RL, rather than assuming exposure and detection are evaluated at a single training snapshot.

2. **Detector Stress Test Across Detection Families**: We evaluate representative contamination detectors spanning reference-based, reference-free, perturbation-based, and generation/output-distribution approaches. This is necessary because prior work such as @mireshghallah2022memorization, @mattern2023neighbourhood, and @dong2024generalization operationalizes contamination through different observables, but all ultimately depend on member/non-member separability. Testing them under identical LRM contamination scenarios reveals whether their shared statistical assumptions fail together.

3. **Algorithmic Isolation of RL Concealment**: We compare rejection-sampling-style RL against PPO-style variants to isolate whether concealment comes from additional clean training or from the RL objective itself. Building on @dong2023raft, we use a plain rejection-sampling baseline without importance-sampling clipping; building on @xiong2025raftpp, we examine the effect of adding PPO-style importance weights and clipping. This controlled comparison is needed to identify the exact training mechanism responsible for shrinking the NLL gap between members and non-members.

4. **Theoretical NLL-Gap Analysis**: We analyze the drift of the member/non-member negative log-likelihood gap under small PPO-style updates, decomposing the effect into a mean advantage term and a covariance term induced by importance sampling and clipping. This component is needed to move beyond empirical observation and explain why PPO-style RL can reduce detectability while preserving reward-improving updates. It extends the algorithmic contrast implied by @dong2023raft and @xiong2025raftpp into a concrete mechanism for contamination concealment.

5. **Stage-II CoT Contamination on Advanced LRMs**: We simulate final-stage contamination by fine-tuning already-advanced reasoning models on benchmark members with chain-of-thought data. This component is needed to test the second failure mode suggested by @jaech2024o1 and the generalization concerns raised by @dong2024generalization: contamination may not produce a member-specific memorization signature if LRMs internalize reasoning structure and transfer it to similar non-members. The evaluation determines whether existing detectors remain informative when contamination behaves like generalized competence rather than literal recall.

Together, these components form a coherent system: Stage I identifies concealment caused by RL optimization, Stage II identifies concealment caused by reasoning-driven generalization, and the detector suite plus theory ties both phenomena back to the failure of member/non-member separability assumptions.
