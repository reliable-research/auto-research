## N1/N2 Motivation (verbatim from corpus record `proposal.motivation`)
Current contamination detection for LLMs is limited by the fact that most methods measure static confidence rather than adaptive behavior. Dataset inference is a more realistic target than per-sample membership inference, since legal and benchmark contamination concerns usually involve collections of related documents rather than isolated sequences, and because sample-level signals are easily confounded by distribution shift, as shown by @maini2024datasetinference. However, existing dataset- and sample-level methods still depend heavily on absolute loss or perplexity, score calibration, perturbations, or external reference models. These choices make detection fragile because raw likelihood is entangled with sequence difficulty, stylistic variation, and mismatch between the suspect data and any auxiliary distribution.

This gap is evident across prior work. @mattern2023neighbourhood shows that stronger membership inference often requires complex comparisons to reference models or synthetically generated neighbors, and that such attacks are sensitive to the choice of comparison distribution. @oren2023contamination improves black-box detection using token-probability statistics, but the broader contamination literature it represents still often relies on prompt-specific assumptions, ordering effects, or other heuristics that are not uniformly available across datasets. At the same time, @maini2024datasetinference indicates that aggregating evidence over datasets can produce reliable inference, suggesting that the remaining challenge is to find a dataset-level statistic that does not require calibration against external models or handcrafted contamination cues.

This creates a concrete opportunity: instead of asking whether a suspect dataset has unusually high likelihood, we can ask how the model's likelihood changes when given additional examples from that same dataset as context. The relevance of this shift follows from two observations in the prior literature. First, @razeghi2022termfreq suggests that few-shot behavior depends on whether patterns are already well represented in pretraining. Second, @dai2023metagrad argues that in-context learning behaves like implicit gradient-based adaptation. If so, unseen datasets should usually provide positive adaptation signal and increase confidence, while memorized datasets should provide little new information and may destabilize memorized token sequences. Measuring this contrast offers a contamination criterion that is relative, model-internal, and dataset-agnostic.

## N6 Methodology (verbatim from corpus record `proposal.method`)
We propose Contamination Detection via Context (CoDeC), a dataset-level contamination detector based on context-induced changes in token log-likelihood. The central idea is to probe a model twice on each sequence—once in isolation and once with demonstrations sampled from the same dataset—and use the sign of the resulting likelihood shift as the contamination signal. This formulation addresses the calibration problem of prior work because it compares the model to itself under a controlled intervention rather than relying on absolute thresholds or external references.

1. **Baseline and Contextual Scoring**: For each target sequence x, we compute the model's average token log-likelihood in two conditions: standalone and preceded by n examples sampled from the same candidate dataset. This isolates the effect of same-distribution context on the exact same prediction target. It extends the reference-free spirit of @oren2023contamination, but replaces static likelihood scoring with an intervention-based comparison.

2. **Delta-Confidence Contamination Signal**: We define a per-sample score as the difference between in-context and baseline log-likelihood, Δ(x). This component is needed because the sign of the change captures whether context behaves like useful few-shot evidence or like interference with memorized continuations. The interpretation is motivated by @razeghi2022termfreq and @dai2023metagrad: adaptation should help on unseen data and saturate or destabilize on memorized data.

3. **Dataset-Level Aggregation**: We aggregate per-sample signals into a contamination score by computing the fraction of examples with Δ(x) < 0. This turns noisy local effects into a stable statistical estimate, directly following the lesson from @maini2024datasetinference that dataset-level inference is more reliable than strict sample-level membership decisions. The resulting score is interpretable as the percentage of dataset samples whose confidence drops under same-dataset context.

4. **Gray-Box, Model-Agnostic Evaluation Protocol**: The method requires only token probabilities from the evaluated model and no access to its training data, no reference model, and no perturbation pipeline. This design is needed to avoid the fragility documented by @mattern2023neighbourhood and the narrow assumptions highlighted by @oren2023contamination. Because it only relies on forward passes under two prompts, it remains practical across architectures and datasets.

5. **Context Scaling and Robust Statistical Use**: The framework naturally supports varying the number of in-context examples and the number of evaluated dataset samples to trade off compute for separation strength. This component is important because contamination is a dataset property, so reliability improves with repeated Bernoulli-style trials over examples. It extends the dataset-inference perspective of @maini2024datasetinference while preserving the simple same-model comparison at the core of CoDeC.

Together, these components form a coherent system: the same-dataset context acts as a controlled adaptation probe, delta log-likelihood converts that probe into a directional signal, and dataset-level aggregation makes the signal stable and interpretable. The result is a simple contamination detector that uses the model's own in-context learning dynamics as evidence of memorization.
