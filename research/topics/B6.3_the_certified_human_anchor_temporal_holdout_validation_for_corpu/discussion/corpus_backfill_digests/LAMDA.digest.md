## N1/N2 Motivation (verbatim from corpus record `proposal.motivation`)
Machine learning–based Android malware detectors have achieved near-perfect performance in controlled experiments, yet these results often fail to generalize in real-world deployments. A key reason is concept drift: the statistical properties of benign and malicious applications evolve over time due to changes in developer practices, Android APIs, and adversarial adaptation by malware authors. Studies such as @pendlebury2019tesseract demonstrated that improper temporal evaluation can significantly inflate performance estimates, while works like @chen2023continuous show that classifiers trained on one year's data can experience drastic performance degradation within months. These findings highlight that temporal distribution shifts are not peripheral but central to the malware detection problem.

Despite growing recognition of concept drift, the datasets used to study it remain inadequate. Existing Android malware datasets—including those used in @pendlebury2019tesseract and @zhang2020apigraph—span relatively short time periods or contain limited numbers of samples and families. As a result, they capture only partial snapshots of malware evolution and cannot support comprehensive longitudinal analysis. Algorithmic approaches for drift mitigation, such as contrastive drift detection in @yang2021cade or selective prediction in @barbero2022transcendent, have therefore been evaluated on datasets that may not fully reflect real-world distribution shifts. This creates a methodological bottleneck: without a dataset explicitly designed for long-term temporal analysis, it is difficult to reliably measure how detection models degrade or to compare adaptation strategies under realistic conditions.

This gap creates an opportunity to design a benchmark that explicitly targets longitudinal concept drift in Android malware. Such a benchmark must combine several properties: large temporal coverage, consistent feature extraction across years, family-level annotations for analyzing evolutionary patterns, and evaluation protocols that simulate increasing temporal distance between training and deployment environments. Providing this infrastructure would enable systematic investigation of drift phenomena, facilitate fair comparison of adaptation methods, and reveal the structural causes of model degradation in evolving malware ecosystems.

## N6 Methodology (verbatim from corpus record `proposal.method`)
We propose LAMDA, a large-scale longitudinal benchmark designed specifically to study concept drift in Android malware detection. The core idea is to construct a temporally structured dataset spanning more than a decade of Android applications while maintaining a consistent feature representation and evaluation protocol. By combining long-term temporal coverage, family-level labeling, and structured drift evaluation, the benchmark enables systematic analysis of distribution shifts and model robustness over time.

1. **Longitudinal Dataset Construction**: We construct a large-scale Android malware dataset spanning 2013–2025 with over one million applications. Each sample is collected from the AndroZoo repository and labeled using VirusTotal detection consensus to ensure consistent labeling practices, following principles used in earlier datasets such as @pendlebury2019tesseract. The extended temporal coverage addresses the limitations of prior datasets such as @zhang2020apigraph by capturing long-term ecosystem evolution and enabling the study of drift across more than a decade.

2. **Consistent Static Feature Representation**: To ensure comparability across time, all applications are decompiled and represented using a unified static feature space derived from Drebin-style features. These include permissions, manifest components, API calls, and network indicators, which are converted into high-dimensional binary vectors. Maintaining a consistent representation across years allows distributional changes to be attributed to genuine ecosystem evolution rather than feature engineering differences, enabling reliable drift analysis similar in spirit to representation-based approaches such as @yang2021cade.

3. **Malware Family Annotation and Evolution Tracking**: Each malware sample is assigned a family label using automated label normalization tools, enabling analysis of malware evolution at the family level. This design enables studies of how behavioral patterns change within and across families over time, an aspect highlighted as important in drift analyses of malware ecosystems. Family-level annotations allow researchers to examine structural causes of drift and understand how emerging families influence classifier performance.

4. **Structured Temporal Evaluation Protocol**: To systematically measure distribution shift, we adopt a temporal evaluation framework inspired by the benchmark methodology of @dragoi2022anoshift. Data are divided into temporally disjoint regions—TRAIN, IID, NEAR, and FAR—allowing models to be evaluated under progressively larger temporal gaps. This protocol provides a standardized way to quantify performance degradation and compare drift mitigation techniques, addressing evaluation concerns raised by @pendlebury2019tesseract.

5. **Comprehensive Drift Analysis Toolkit**: Beyond dataset construction, the benchmark includes analytical tools for examining feature distribution shifts, latent-space drift, and explanation dynamics. Techniques such as representation-based drift detection inspired by @yang2021cade and model behavior analysis related to studies like @barbero2022transcendent are used to reveal how classifier reasoning changes over time.

Together, these components create a unified benchmark infrastructure that supports realistic evaluation of malware detection systems under long-term concept drift while enabling deeper analysis of the structural causes of distributional change.
