## N2 Motivation (verbatim from proposal.motivation)

Existing work suggests that LLMs contain confidence-related and self-referential information, but it remains unclear whether they can use that information to guide behavior. @kadavath2022know showed that token probabilities and elicited confidence judgments correlate with correctness, establishing an implicit confidence signal. However, calibration alone does not demonstrate metacognitive control: a model may expose uncertainty in its output distribution without deploying that uncertainty when a decision depends on it. Similarly, @binder2024introspection provided evidence that models can learn to predict properties of their own behavior from privileged internal access, but this required finetuning and degraded on more complex tasks, leaving open whether unmodified frontier models behaviorally exploit self-knowledge in novel settings.

A second gap concerns self-modeling under decision pressure. @chen2023selfconsistency found that LLMs often fail to predict or remain consistent with their own outputs in hypothetical contexts, suggesting weak access to their own future responses. At the same time, @griot2025metacognition showed that models can be highly capable on domain tasks while still failing to recognize limits or modulate confidence appropriately, which is precisely the failure mode that makes metacognition practically important. These results indicate that current evaluations are often too dependent on verbal reports, explicit introspection prompts, or post hoc calibration metrics. They do not cleanly test whether a model can access internal signals that are not directly available to an external observer and convert them into better actions.

This gap matters because behavioral use of internal signals is a stronger and more safety-relevant notion of metacognition than self-description. If LLMs can monitor confidence or anticipate their own outputs, they should gain measurable advantage in tasks where acting on that information improves reward. Conversely, if they cannot, then strong self-reports may be largely imitative rather than evidential. Inspired by benchmark-style operationalization in @laine2024sad and incentive-based self-reasoning evaluations in @fronsdal2024misr, we propose to measure metacognition through controlled games in which exploiting internal information is useful but never explicitly requested.

## N6 Methodology (verbatim from proposal.method)

We propose a behavioral evaluation framework with two complementary game paradigms: one for confidence-based control and one for self-modeling-based control. Rather than asking models to report confidence or introspect verbally, the framework infers metacognition from whether models improve outcomes in incentive-compatible tasks where internal signals should be useful. This formulation addresses the core gap left by calibration and self-report methods by testing strategic deployment of privileged information.

1. **Delegate Game for confidence-based control**: The model is presented with difficult questions and must choose either to answer directly or delegate to a teammate, with payoff determined by total team accuracy. This component is needed to convert confidence from a descriptive signal into an action policy: if the model has access to an internal uncertainty estimate, it should answer selectively and delegate when subjectively uncertain. It builds on the calibration findings of @kadavath2022know and the strategic framing of @fronsdal2024misr, but replaces explicit confidence reports with behavior.

2. **Contextual teammate modeling**: The prompt includes a prior interaction phase showing the teammate’s past performance, allowing the model to estimate teammate reliability from in-context evidence. This is necessary because rational delegation depends not just on self-confidence but on comparing expected self-performance to estimated teammate performance. It extends the benchmarking logic of @laine2024sad by embedding self-knowledge inside a mixed self/other decision problem rather than testing it in isolation.

3. **Proxy-based analysis of internal confidence signals**: We analyze delegation decisions against baseline correctness and, when available, entropy over answer token probabilities while controlling for surface cues such as topic, format, question length, and apparent difficulty. This component is needed to distinguish genuine use of internal signals from reliance on externally observable heuristics. It directly operationalizes the confidence signal hypothesized by @kadavath2022know and is further motivated by @jian2025metacognitive, which suggests that monitorable internal activations may underlie such downstream behavior.

4. **Second Chance Game for self-modeling**: The model is shown a previously answered question, told that its earlier answer was wrong, and asked to answer again without being shown the original response. This component tests whether the model can anticipate its own likely answer and use that prediction to revise behavior, addressing the failure mode identified by @chen2023selfconsistency. It also avoids the finetuning dependence seen in @binder2024introspection by probing self-modeling in zero-shot behavioral form.

5. **Alternative-strategy controls and neutral baselines**: We compare answer-change rates against a neutral re-answering prompt and test competing explanations such as random guessing, choosing implausible options, or injecting noise. This is needed because behavioral success can otherwise be mimicked by non-metacognitive strategies. It responds directly to the concerns raised by @griot2025metacognition that apparent confidence-sensitive behavior may mask poor underlying metacognitive control.

Together, these components form a coherent system: the Delegate Game measures whether models use internal confidence to allocate action, and the Second Chance Game measures whether they can model and regulate their own outputs. The combined design isolates metacognitive behavior from self-report and provides quantitative evidence about both the presence and the limits of LLM metacognition.
