# How Few Replays Suffice? Budget-Bounded Localization of Decisive Failures in LLM-Agent Trajectories

## Motivation

When a long LLM-agent trajectory fails, the final error rarely reveals the step that actually caused the failure. A wrong plan may only surface several tool calls later; a malformed tool invocation may be partially repaired by later actions; and an execution-level mistake may hide behind a plausible but unsuccessful final response. Agents now routinely chain dozens of planning, tool-use, and environment-interaction steps, so for a developer the practical debugging question is not *whether* the trajectory failed, but *where the decisive failure occurred* — and in which layer, plan, tool, or execution.

The common response is to inspect the full interaction log. But log inspection is expensive, inconsistent, and fundamentally correlational: a step that looks suspicious need not be the step that changed the outcome. The causally grounded alternative is **counterfactual replay** — resume the trajectory from an intermediate checkpoint, replay the suffix, and check whether the task's own verifier still fails. If replay from before a step can recover but replay from after it still fails, that step becomes a verified decisive-failure point. Replay turns attribution from post-hoc explanation into an intervention-based procedure.

The problem is that counterfactual replay is expensive. Each replay re-invokes the LLM, tools, and environment, and replaying every checkpoint scales linearly with trajectory length. Existing failure-attribution work either avoids replay and relies on log-only judgment, or uses replay exhaustively to generate verified labels — and neither answers the key engineering quantity: **how many replays are actually needed to localize the decisive failure step under a fixed debugging budget?** No prior system's evaluation characterizes how much of that linear cost a smarter search could have saved. That uncharacterized cost-accuracy tradeoff is the object of this study.

Our central observation is that a decisive failure often creates a **state-inheritance boundary**: checkpoints *before* the failure do not carry the corrupted agent/environment state, while checkpoints *after* it inherit it. Under this structure the replay outcome `g(k)` — the verifier's pass/fail result of replaying from checkpoint `k` — behaves approximately like a step function over checkpoint index, which suggests that structured search such as bisection or group testing could localize the failure with far fewer replays than exhaustive scanning. But this shortcut is only valid when the replay signal is sufficiently monotone and stable. Long trajectories can recover from earlier mistakes, introduce later independent failures, or produce stochastic replay outcomes. The scientific question is therefore not merely whether structured replay can be faster, but **when the state-inheritance assumption holds strongly enough to make sublinear replay localization reliable** — a question we answer by *measuring* where it breaks, not by assuming it away.

## Introduction

A failed agent trajectory tells us the task did not succeed; it does not tell us which of its dozens of steps made success impossible. Developers must inspect long traces and infer whether the decisive error arose from an early plan, a wrong tool call, a malformed argument, an execution mistake, or a later recovery failure — a process that is costly, subjective, and hard to scale. As LLM agents take on longer-horizon software, web, and tool-use tasks, this per-failure debugging cost is becoming a routine bottleneck rather than an occasional one.

The capability this paper studies is **causally verified failure localization**: given a failed trajectory, identify the decisive step and its layer under a limited replay budget. This is stricter than log-based attribution. A log-only judge can point at a suspicious step but cannot verify whether changing it would have changed the outcome — and the field's own numbers bear this out: step-level log attribution is reported to be substantially worse than agent-level attribution, precisely because a judge that never re-executes anything can only guess. Counterfactual replay provides the stronger signal by resuming from a checkpoint and re-executing the suffix under the task's own verifier.

But current replay-based attribution leaves a resource question open. Exhaustive replay tests every checkpoint, and its cost grows linearly with trajectory length — tens to hundreds of expensive invocations per failure. Worse, replay cost is **position-dependent**: replaying from an early checkpoint re-executes most of the trajectory, while replaying from a late one re-executes only a short suffix. Counting "number of probes" therefore misprices the true debugging cost. What practitioners need is a **cost-accuracy curve**: for a fixed replay budget, how accurately can the decisive step be localized, and how close to exhaustive replay can a structured search get?

Our key insight is the state-inheritance boundary: once a failure corrupts state, later checkpoints inherit it and earlier ones do not, so the idealized `g(k)` is close to a threshold function over checkpoint index. A bisection localizer can search for that boundary in logarithmically many probes, and group-testing designs add redundancy when replay outcomes are noisy or when a failure's cause and symptom cross plan/tool/execution boundaries. We stress that these are classical search *instruments*, not a new algorithm — the contribution is a **measurement of how much this structure actually buys, and where it silently fails.** This distinction also separates our question from the nearest-looking prior work: Who&When applies binary search too, but over *the log context an LLM judge reads*, which never re-executes anything and so carries no causal guarantee; we bisect over *the checkpoints that get re-executed and outcome-verified*, so what our search narrows is a causally grounded boundary, not a judge's reading order.

The shortcut is not guaranteed. Agent trajectories are not deterministic programs: later steps may recover from earlier errors, a second independent error may dominate the outcome, and repeated replays from the same checkpoint may disagree under stochastic decoding or nondeterministic tools. These cases violate the monotonicity that makes bisection attractive. A useful characterization must therefore not merely apply structured search; it must measure *when* structured search is safe, when it silently breaks, and when exhaustive or hypothesis-then-verify replay is still necessary.

We study this on trajectories with programmatically injected, replay-verified decisive failures balanced across plan, tool, and execution layers, in a deterministic household-task simulator and a cached-web tool-use domain, with a human-annotated natural-failure slice reported separately so that any gap between injected and natural failures is exposed rather than averaged away. We define replay cost in **resumed-LLM-call units** rather than raw probe counts, so savings reflect actual suffix length, and we run bisection, noise-tolerant group testing, exhaustive per-step replay, budget-matched uniform sampling, and zero-replay log-only judging on the *same* trajectories at the *same* replay-unit budgets. We further measure the **monotonicity-violation rate** of each replay profile, turning the premise behind structured search into an explicit diagnostic variable rather than an unstated assumption.

Against the exhaustive scan that current practice uses to generate verified labels, our headline pre-registered target is that, **on the replay-verified injected corpus**, bisection recovers **`[TARGET: ≥90%]`** of exhaustive exact-step accuracy while spending only **`[TARGET: ≤25%]`** of its replay-unit cost — an effective **`[TARGET: ~4× (Y×)]`** cost reduction, scaling toward **`[TARGET: Θ(log n / n)]`** as length grows. At a fixed budget of `B = n/4`, structured search is targeted to beat budget-matched uniform sampling by **`[TARGET: ≥15 pts]`** exact-step accuracy and sit **`[TARGET: ≥20 pts]`** above the zero-replay judge. Under higher replay noise, group testing is targeted to improve accuracy-per-replay-unit over vote-multiplied bisection once the single-replay flip rate exceeds **`[TARGET: ≥15%]`**. Crucially, we do not assume the step-function structure holds: the monotonicity-violation rate is targeted to shrink bisection's advantage over uniform sampling to within **`[TARGET: ≤5 pts]`** precisely on the violating stratum, giving practitioners a measurable go/no-go signal for when cheap structured replay is reliable. Because these are pre-registered targets, each disconfirming outcome is a clean finding routed to a pre-committed fallback, not a result rewritten after the fact.

## Contributions

1. **A resource-centered formulation of agent failure localization.** We define budget-bounded decisive-failure localization and account for replay cost using position-dependent resumed-call units rather than raw probe counts, showing that a comparison ignoring *where* a probe lands misprices every strategy it compares.
2. **The monotonicity-violation rate as a measured, falsifiable diagnostic.** We turn the assumption that makes bisection efficient into a measured quantity and test it, via a pre-registered violating/non-violating stratification, as the predictor of where the shortcut holds versus where it silently breaks.
3. **A characterization of the noise and layer regimes.** We measure the oracle-noise flip rate (**`[TARGET: ≥15%]`**) above which redundancy-based group testing overtakes vote-multiplied bisection, and the layer regime in which crossing plan/tool/execution boundaries in a partition localizes failures contiguous partitions miss.
4. **The first matched-budget, head-to-head cost-accuracy comparison** of structured replay against exhaustive per-step replay, budget-matched uniform sampling, zero-replay log-only judging, and a hypothesis-then-verify baseline, on replay-verified failures across all three layers — establishing whether, and by how much (**`[TARGET: ≥90% accuracy at ≤25% cost]`**), index structure and replay access each do real work, plus a reusable replay harness and replay-verified corpus.

## Core claim (positioning line)

> Counterfactual replay is causal but expensive; this paper measures *when* its cost can be reduced from exhaustive per-step scanning to budget-bounded structured search over re-executed checkpoints, and *when* recovery-induced non-monotonicity makes that shortcut unsafe.
