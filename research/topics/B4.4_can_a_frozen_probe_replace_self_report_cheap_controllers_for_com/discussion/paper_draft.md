# Paid From the Same Budget It Spends: Matched-Budget Pricing of Frozen Probes, Self-Report, and Training-Free Compute-Allocation Controllers

> Assembled from the six confirmed stage outputs in `pipeline.md`, post-audit (`taste_audit.md`: 🟢). Status: **specification / paper draft — no experiments have been run.** All numbers below are pre-registered design targets, not results.

## Abstract

Adaptive test-time compute allocation for large language models relies on a per-query controller signal to decide whether a query deserves a cheap or an expensive execution tier — and that signal itself must be paid for out of the same budget it is trying to protect. Self-report costs one extra large-model call and inherits that model's calibration failures; self-consistency-style training-free signals cost k−1 extra samples before any budget is even saved; a probe reading a frozen backbone's already-computed prefill hidden state costs almost nothing, because the forward pass it reads from has already happened for an unrelated reason. No prior comparison charges each of these controller families its own signal-acquisition cost against one shared dollar budget: recent probing studies already show hidden-state probes can rival or beat verbalized confidence as raw uncertainty signals, but none prices the signal as a routing decision, so it remains unknown whether the probe's cheaper signal actually buys better routing at equal realized spend, or merely a cheaper meter reading. We meter every controller inside one threshold-swept, precompute-then-simulate router; recycle already-required self-consistency samples as probe training labels at no added annotation cost; guard the self-report baseline with two independently tuned elicitation variants; and isolate genuine decision-quality gains from budget arithmetic via a cost-free signal-quality contrast. We pre-register matched-budget non-inferiority, training-free-sufficiency, and cross-task/cross-backbone transfer criteria, and report which controller family a fixed dollar actually buys the most accuracy from.

## Introduction

Large language models increasingly spend a variable amount of test-time compute per query — more reasoning tokens, more sampled attempts, a larger backbone — and someone, or something, has to decide how much a given query deserves before its difficulty is known. This per-query allocation decision is made by a controller: a signal computed from the query, and sometimes a first cheap attempt at it, that a routing policy thresholds to decide whether to spend more. As test-time compute scaling becomes standard deployment practice, this controller is no longer an implementation detail; it is itself a line item in the deployment budget.

Yet the controller signal is rarely free, and its cost is almost never charged against the very budget it is supposed to be protecting. Two controller families dominate current practice, and both must spend some of that budget before they can save any of it: eliciting a large model's own self-reported confidence costs one additional generation call and inherits whatever calibration failures that model has; estimating agreement via self-consistency sampling costs k−1 additional samples before a single routing decision is made. A third candidate signal is different in kind: a probe trained on a frozen backbone's hidden state, read off a prefill pass that has already happened for an unrelated reason — producing the cheap tier's own draft answer — costs almost nothing to query at decision time. If that near-free signal's routing decisions are genuinely as good as self-report's, the controller stops being a budget item at all; if they are not, that is equally worth knowing, because it tells a deployer that self-report's extra cost is buying something real.

The nearest existing work already builds exactly this probe. DiffAdapt trains a lightweight classifier on a frozen backbone's post-prefill hidden state to route queries into easy/normal/hard reasoning configurations, and AdaptiveThinking trains a similar head to predict a query's self-consistency score from the same prefill representation — both are the closest prior work to the probe studied here, and the precise delta is what neither of them does: each validates its probe against fixed-budget or heuristic reasoning baselines within one backbone's own reasoning modes, reporting token savings at preserved accuracy, but neither puts that probe head-to-head against large-model self-report, and neither reports a matched-dollar-budget frame in which every controller's own signal cost is charged. Adjacent lines of work close different, related gaps: cascade-routing systems such as DiSRouter and TRIM train models to decide when to defer to a stronger tier, but at the cost of a per-model or per-step training investment a frozen probe is built to avoid; Kadavath et al.'s self-report protocol establishes that a model's own verbalized or logprob-based confidence correlates with correctness, without pricing the elicitation call itself; and router-evaluation platforms such as RouterArena benchmark routers that choose between models, not within-model compute tiers, and do not treat entropy, self-consistency, probes, and self-report as interchangeable signals under one shared budget.

Turning "is the probe as good as self-report" into an answerable question surfaces several difficulties of its own. Probe training needs correctness-labeled queries, and if obtaining those labels cost anything beyond what the weakest baseline already pays, the comparison would measure label budget rather than controller quality. The three controller families are priced in genuinely different native units — an amortized training cost, k−1 extra samples, one extra call — and converting all of them into one dollar meter risks a ranking that flips depending on which of two defensible cost models, API list price or GPU-seconds, is used. A probe trained on one task and one frozen backbone might simply not transfer, since whatever separability it exploits could be an artifact of that specific backbone, layer, and task distribution. Self-report itself has no single canonical elicitation form, so an unprincipled choice between its two common variants could make the probe look better than it is merely by picking a weak self-report baseline. And even a well-priced matched-budget win could be a mechanical artifact of cost alone: a cheaper signal wins by construction if it simply leaves more of the shared budget for expensive-tier generation, whether or not its actual decisions are any good.

Our approach turns each of these obstacles into a specific design choice rather than a hedge. Probe training labels are recycled from the cheap-tier samples the self-consistency baseline already needs, so probe supervision adds training compute but no new annotation cost. A single offline threshold-sweep simulator meters every controller's own signal-acquisition cost under two disclosed cost models and compares all three families at identical realized spend across a pre-registered grid of budget points, rather than at one hand-picked operating point. Cross-task and cross-backbone evaluation of an unretrained probe measures, rather than assumes away, whatever transfer gap the frozen-feature design produces. Two independently tuned self-report variants are compared before any evaluation result is seen, and the stronger is used as the headline baseline, so the comparison cannot be won by a strawmanned elicitation. And a budget-independent signal-quality contrast, computed identically for every controller, checks whether a matched-budget win reflects genuinely better decisions or merely a cheaper signal — reporting the mechanical explanation explicitly if that is what the evidence shows.

We evaluate this design on exact-answer reasoning benchmarks with programmatically scorable ground truth, using a precompute-then-simulate harness that caches both execution tiers and every controller signal once, so every routing policy and budget point can be replayed offline against the same cached outputs.

In summary, we contribute:
- An identification and formalization of the cost-commensurability gap in compute-allocation controller comparisons: existing evaluations of frozen probes, self-report, and training-free signals never charge each controller its own signal-acquisition cost against one shared dollar budget, making any claimed controller advantage unpriced.
- A matched-budget, threshold-swept comparison harness that recycles already-required self-consistency samples as probe-training labels at no added annotation cost, and meters every controller's signal cost under two disclosed cost models.
- A construct-validity safeguard for the self-report baseline — two independently tuned elicitation variants, the stronger reported as headline — together with a budget-independent decision-quality isolation test that separates genuine signal quality from budget arithmetic.
- An empirical account, in whichever direction the evidence falls, of which controller family a fixed dollar buys the most accuracy from at matched realized spend, and a characterization of how far that advantage, if any, survives cross-task and cross-backbone transfer.

## Problem Definition

**Estimand.** The quantity of interest is $\max_\pi \text{Acc}_\pi(\tau_\pi(B))$ — matched-realized-budget accuracy — compared across controller families $\pi \in \{\text{training-free}, \text{probe}, \text{self-report}\}$ at a pre-registered grid of total dollar budgets $B$, where the unit of analysis is one (query, controller, budget-point) triple and the decision the estimand feeds is whether a near-zero-marginal-cost frozen probe can replace self-report/training-free signals as a compute-allocation controller; RQ1–RQ3 below operationalize this single estimand into three binary tests.

**Formal setup.** Consider a query set $Q$ and two execution tiers $t \in \{\text{cheap}, \text{exp}\}$, each with programmatic correctness $y_t(q) \in \{0,1\}$ and a measured dollar cost $c_t(q)$. A **controller** $\pi$ emits a per-query signal $s_\pi(q) \in \mathbb{R}$ at its own acquisition cost $a_\pi(q)$. A threshold routing policy sends a query to the expensive tier iff $s_\pi(q) < \tau$. At a target total budget $B$, the threshold $\tau_\pi(B)$ is chosen so that the controller's realized total cost, $\text{Cost}_\pi(\tau) = \sum_q \left[ a_\pi(q) + c_{\text{cheap}}(q) + \mathbb{1}[s_\pi(q)<\tau]\,c_{\text{exp}}(q) \right]$, equals $B$. The resulting realized accuracy is $\text{Acc}_\pi(\tau_\pi(B)) = \frac{1}{|Q|}\sum_q \left[ \mathbb{1}[s_\pi(q)<\tau]\,y_{\text{exp}}(q) + \mathbb{1}[s_\pi(q)\ge\tau]\,y_{\text{cheap}}(q) \right]$. The object of study is, across $\pi \in \{\text{training-free}, \text{probe}, \text{self-report}\}$ and a pre-registered grid of budgets $B$, which controller maximizes $\text{Acc}_\pi(\tau_\pi(B))$ — and specifically whether the near-zero-cost probe is non-inferior to self-report.

**Why this is hard.** Fixing $Q$, the two tiers, and a shared budget does not by itself make $\text{Acc}_\pi(\tau_\pi(B))$ comparable across controllers, because $a_\pi(q)$ is measured in genuinely different native units for each $\pi$ — an amortized training cost for the probe, $k-1$ extra samples for self-consistency, one extra generation call for self-report — and folding all three into a single $\text{Cost}_\pi$ risks a ranking that depends on which of two defensible conversions (API list price vs. measured GPU-seconds) is used to turn each into dollars. The probe's signal $s_\pi(q)$ is itself only available once labeled training queries exist, and if those labels cost anything beyond what the weakest baseline (self-consistency) already pays to compute, the resulting comparison would be measuring label budget rather than $\text{Acc}_\pi$ itself. Because the probe is trained on features from one backbone and one query distribution, its separability may not transfer to a different task or backbone, so any $\text{Acc}_{\text{probe}}(\tau(B))$ advantage observed in-distribution is not yet evidence about $\text{Acc}_{\text{probe}}$ under distribution shift. Self-report has no single canonical operationalization of $s_\pi(q)$, so an unprincipled choice between its variants could inflate or deflate $\text{Acc}_{\text{self-report}}(\tau(B))$ independent of the underlying comparison. And because $\text{Cost}_\pi$ enters $\tau_\pi(B)$ directly, a controller with lower $a_\pi(q)$ mechanically routes more queries to the expensive tier at the same $B$ — so an observed gap in $\text{Acc}_\pi(\tau_\pi(B))$ between two controllers can reflect a difference in $a_\pi(q)$ alone, not a difference in how well $s_\pi(q)$ actually ranks queries by difficulty.

## Method Overview

At a conceptual level, the study is a single measurement pipeline with five cooperating parts, organized around one offline cache: every query's cheap-tier samples, expensive-tier output, and all three controllers' raw signals are computed once, so any threshold $\tau$ and any budget $B$ can be replayed exactly without re-running a model. A recycled-label pipeline turns the cheap tier's own required samples into probe training supervision at no added annotation cost. A dual-cost-metered router turns the cached signals and costs into a matched-realized-budget accuracy table for every controller, under two disclosed cost models. A cross-distribution transfer design turns the same cache, extended to held-out tasks and a second backbone, into a measured (not assumed) transfer gap. A dual self-report operationalization turns the choice of "which elicitation is the baseline" into a pre-registered, dev-tuned decision rather than an arbitrary one. And a cost-free decision-quality isolation turns the matched-budget accuracy table into a check on whether any controller's apparent advantage is a genuine signal-quality effect or a mechanical consequence of its acquisition cost alone.

The **recycled-label pipeline** takes the cheap-tier samples already required for the self-consistency baseline and reuses their empirical correctness as probe training labels, on a held-out, de-duplicated query pool. The **dual-cost-metered router** takes every controller's raw signal and per-query acquisition cost and produces, for a pre-registered grid of budgets, the threshold and resulting accuracy that make each controller's realized spend identical. The **cross-distribution probe transfer** design takes the resulting probe, frozen after training on one benchmark, and evaluates its unretrained routing quality on held-out benchmarks and a second backbone family. The **dual self-report operationalization** takes two independently prompt-tuned elicitation variants and fixes, before any evaluation result is seen, which one is reported as the headline self-report baseline. The **cost-free decision-quality isolation** takes each controller's raw signal and computes its budget-independent AUROC against realized correctness, checked against the matched-budget ranking for directional agreement.

## Methodology

### Recycled-Label Pipeline

**Challenge.** Probe training needs correctness-labeled queries, and if obtaining those labels cost anything beyond what the weakest baseline already pays, the resulting comparison would measure label budget rather than controller quality.

**Insight.** The only correctness signal available at zero incremental annotation cost is exactly the signal the self-consistency baseline already needs for its own agreement-rate statistic — reusing it means the probe's added cost is training compute alone, not label acquisition.

**Method.** A logistic-regression head and a 2-layer MLP head are trained on the final-token hidden state of the frozen cheap-tier backbone at prefill, using empirical correctness computed from the k cached cheap-tier samples on a held-out training query pool that is disjoint from, and de-duplicated against, the evaluation set. Because these samples are the same ones the self-consistency baseline consumes, probe supervision is a byproduct of a computation the weakest baseline already performs.

### Dual-Cost-Metered Router

**Challenge.** The three controller families are priced in genuinely different native units, and converting them into one dollar meter risks a ranking that flips depending on which cost model is used.

**Insight.** Because every controller's own signal cost is charged before any routing benefit is counted, a controller can only win by making the shared remaining budget go further — either through cheaper signal acquisition or through better-targeted routing decisions — and reporting two disclosed cost models lets a reader check whether the ranking depends on which one is used.

**Method.** A shared thresholded router sweeps each controller's signal over its own range in an offline simulator. For every target total budget, the threshold is chosen so that controller's realized cost — its own signal-acquisition cost plus routed generation cost, computed under both a public API list-price model and a measured GPU-seconds model — equals that budget, and all controllers are compared at that identical realized spend.

### Cross-Distribution Probe Transfer

**Challenge.** A probe trained on one task and one frozen backbone might simply not transfer, since whatever separability it exploits could be an artifact of that specific backbone, layer, and task distribution.

**Insight.** A no-retraining floor — the training-free router, which needs no probe-specific training at all — is the right comparator for a transfer test, because it isolates whether the probe's frozen-feature advantage survives distribution shift specifically, rather than comparing two numbers that could both move for unrelated reasons.

**Method.** The probe is trained once on one Stage-1 benchmark's recycled labels and evaluated, unchanged, on held-out benchmarks and a second backbone family. The resulting matched-budget accuracy is compared against the training-free self-consistency router evaluated on the same held-out conditions, reporting the transfer gap directly rather than only an in-distribution number.

### Dual Self-Report Operationalization

**Challenge.** Self-report has no single canonical elicitation form, so an unprincipled choice between its two common variants could make one controller look better than it is merely by picking a weak baseline.

**Insight.** Fixing which self-report variant is "the" baseline before seeing evaluation results, and doing so by picking the empirically stronger variant on a held-out dev split, is what prevents the comparison's headline finding from being an artifact of an unlucky or convenient baseline choice.

**Method.** Two self-report elicitation variants — verbalized 0–100 confidence and a P(True)-style logprob readout — are each prompt-tuned on a dev split disjoint from the evaluation set. The stronger of the two on that dev split is fixed, before any evaluation-set result is computed, as the headline self-report baseline in every subsequent comparison; both variants are still reported to avoid construct lock-in.

### Cost-Free Decision-Quality Isolation

**Challenge.** Even a well-priced matched-budget win could be a mechanical artifact of cost alone: a cheaper signal wins by construction if it simply leaves more of the shared budget for expensive-tier generation, whether or not its actual decisions are any good.

**Insight.** A benign budget-arithmetic win moves the matched-budget ranking without necessarily moving a budget-independent signal-quality ranking; requiring the two to agree in direction is exactly the condition that separates "the probe makes better decisions" from "the probe is just cheaper."

**Method.** Each controller's raw signal is scored by its AUROC against cheap-tier correctness, computed identically for every controller with no cost accounting at all. This budget-independent ranking is compared against the matched-budget accuracy ranking; if the two disagree — a controller wins on matched-budget accuracy despite a lower AUROC — that win is reported explicitly as a cost-mechanical effect rather than a decision-quality finding.

## Experiments

### Experimental Setup

**Datasets.** GSM8K — pilot stage; exact-match numeric answers give programmatic scoring for free. MMLU-Pro — pilot stage; 10-option multiple choice, stresses the predictive-entropy training-free variant and gives a second Stage-1 domain for the corrected significance tests. MATH-500 and GPQA-Diamond — Stage 2 held-out benchmarks used specifically to test whether the probe's transfer gap is bounded, rather than only being observed post hoc on the two fixed Stage-1 corpora. BIG-Bench-Hard — Stage 2; a multi-task suite whose component tasks differ from GSM8K/MMLU-Pro in *task format*, not just topic, letting the transfer research question test whether the probe's transfer gap is driven by topic shift alone or by format shift as well, rather than treating "held-out benchmark" as one undifferentiated condition.

**Baselines** (sanity-check comparators, not competitors to beat — this is a measurement study, and no research question here is framed as "our procedure beats baseline X"). A random router at matched budget is the necessary "why do this at all" control: if no real controller beats it on accuracy-per-dollar, per-query signal is worthless and the whole comparison is void. All-cheap and all-expensive fixed policies anchor the budget endpoints, and an oracle router (routes to the expensive tier iff the cheap tier is wrong) anchors the upper bound. The logit/predictive-entropy router and the self-consistency router (k=8) are the training-free family under test. The verbalized-confidence and P(True)-style self-report routers are the self-report family under test, with the stronger reported as headline per the construct-validity safeguard above.

**Metrics.** Matched-budget accuracy and accuracy-per-dollar per controller across the budget grid; area under the cost–accuracy curve, normalized between the all-cheap and oracle endpoints; each raw signal's AUROC against cheap-tier correctness; and 15-bin expected calibration error for the probe's and self-report's output probabilities.

**Research questions:**
1. RQ1 — Does a probe trained on recycled self-consistency labels carry non-trivial routing signal at all?
2. RQ2 — Is the frozen probe non-inferior, and where superior, to the strongest self-report variant at matched realized budget?
3. RQ3 — Are training-free signals alone equivalent to the best controller once their own sampling cost is charged?
4. RQ4 — Does the probe's routing quality transfer across held-out tasks and backbones?
5. RQ5 — Is the dual-self-report-variant safeguard load-bearing, or would a single variant have given the same verdict?
6. RQ6 — Does a matched-budget win reflect genuine decision quality, or budget arithmetic alone?

#### RQ1: Does a probe trained on recycled self-consistency labels carry non-trivial routing signal at all?

**Setup.** Train the probe (logistic-regression and 2-layer-MLP heads) solely on labels recycled from cached k=8 self-consistency samples on a held-out, de-duplicated GSM8K training pool (~5,000 queries); compute AUROC against cheap-tier correctness on a held-out GSM8K dev split.

**Expected outcome.** Probe AUROC exceeds 0.60 against cheap-tier correctness — the minimum separability needed for the probe to be a non-trivial routing signal at all, and the walking-skeleton gate this whole comparison depends on.

**Falsification condition.** Refuted if probe AUROC is at or below 0.60 (indistinguishable from a near-chance detector) on the GSM8K dev split — in which case the probe is not a viable controller and the remaining research questions involving it (RQ2, RQ4–RQ6) cannot be meaningfully run.

**Hypothesis (plain language).** A frozen-backbone probe trained only on labels that cost nothing beyond what the self-consistency baseline already pays for still carries real, usable difficulty signal.

**Metrics.** AUROC(probe signal, cheap-tier correctness) on held-out GSM8K queries.

#### RQ2: Is the frozen probe non-inferior, and where superior, to the strongest self-report variant at matched realized budget?

**Setup.** For every tested (benchmark × budget) cell across GSM8K and MMLU-Pro at 5 pre-registered budget points, compute matched-budget accuracy for the probe and for the stronger self-report variant (per RQ5's safeguard) using the same cached outputs; paired McNemar test on per-query correctness at each cell, α=0.05, Benjamini–Hochberg corrected across the 10 (5 budgets × 2 benchmarks) cells. Sample size: n=2,000 paired evaluation queries per benchmark, sized for ≈80% power at a ≈3-percentage-point accuracy gap. The non-inferiority margin is set at −2pp, matching RQ3's TOST margin, on explicit power arithmetic: the paired-bootstrap 95% CI half-width on the accuracy gap at n=2,000 is ≈1.96·√(d/n) ≈ 1.2–2.0pp at plausible discordance rates d of 8–20%, so a −1pp margin could not be excluded even under a true zero gap — a margin narrower than the design's resolvable width would render the non-inferiority claim structurally unanswerable rather than testable. Any cell whose realized discordant-pair count after routing falls below 30 pairs is pre-declared INCONCLUSIVE (never null, never silently dropped) and excluded from both tallies below.

**Expected outcome.** The probe is *non-inferior* to the strongest self-report variant at all 10 cells: McNemar does not reject in self-report's favor (BH-corrected) *and* the paired-bootstrap 95% CI on the accuracy gap does not exclude −2pp in self-report's favor — i.e. any loss stays within the 2pp practical-equivalence margin justified in the Setup's power arithmetic. The probe is *strictly superior* at ≥2 of the 5 budget points on each benchmark: McNemar rejects in the probe's favor (BH α=0.05) *and* the paired-bootstrap 95% CI on the accuracy gap excludes 0 with a point estimate ≥1pp — the effect floor below which a nominally significant win is not counted toward the tally (guidance.md §3.1: significance without a stated magnitude floor passes nothing).

**Falsification condition.** Refuted toward self-report if McNemar rejects in self-report's favor, BH-corrected, at a majority of the 10 cells — i.e., expensive-but-informed self-report beats cheap-but-frozen probing even after paying for its own elicitation call. If refuted, the fallback claim becomes a cost-crossover characterization: "self-report retains a matched-budget accuracy edge of X percentage points below budget B*; the probe is the accuracy-per-dollar choice only above B*" — still a reportable frontier result, not a null outcome.

**Hypothesis (plain language).** A near-zero-cost signal read off a computation that already happened matches, and sometimes beats, an extra model call built specifically to elicit confidence.

**Metrics.** Matched-budget accuracy and its McNemar test statistic per cell; accuracy-per-dollar; area under the cost–accuracy curve.

#### RQ3: Are training-free signals alone equivalent to the best controller once their own sampling cost is charged?

**Setup.** For logit/predictive entropy and self-consistency agreement (k=8) separately, run a TOST equivalence test (±2 percentage points) against the best controller's matched-budget accuracy at each of the 10 (budget × benchmark) cells, BH-corrected; separately test whether the probe's matched-budget accuracy exceeds each training-free controller by more than 2 percentage points (paired-bootstrap 95% CI, 10,000 resamples) once self-consistency's k−1 extra samples are charged. Cells are pre-declared adequately powered for the ±2pp margin at n=2,000 by construction of the margin choice; any cell whose realized discordant-pair count after routing falls below 30 pairs (a sparse threshold sweep near the budget extremes) is pre-declared INCONCLUSIVE — neither equivalent nor non-equivalent — and excluded from the ≥3-of-5 tally rather than counted either way.

**Expected outcome.** Neither training-free controller passes the ±2pp TOST equivalence test against the best controller at ≥3 of the 5 budget points on either benchmark, and the probe exceeds each training-free controller by more than 2 percentage points at ≥3 of the 5 points.

**Falsification condition.** Refuted (training-free signals already suffice) if either training-free controller passes the ±2pp TOST equivalence test against the best controller at a majority of cells — in which case the seed's own "are training-free signals sufficient?" question is answered "yes," and the probe's marginal value is reported as too small to justify its training pipeline, a negative-for-probe but decision-relevant-for-practice result.

**Hypothesis (plain language).** Once self-consistency's own sampling cost is priced in, training-free signals alone leave real accuracy-per-dollar on the table relative to the best controller.

**Metrics.** TOST equivalence bound crossing per cell; paired-bootstrap 95% CI on the probe-vs-training-free accuracy gap.

#### RQ4: Does the probe's routing quality transfer across held-out tasks and backbones?

**Setup.** Train the probe once on GSM8K's recycled labels; evaluate its unretrained routing quality on at least 2 of 3 held-out benchmarks (MATH-500, GPQA-Diamond, BIG-Bench-Hard) and on a second backbone family (Llama-3.1), comparing matched-budget accuracy against the training-free self-consistency router evaluated under the same held-out conditions; paired-bootstrap 95% CI on the transfer gap per held-out benchmark. Any held-out benchmark whose routed-expensive-tier subset — the only queries whose transfer accuracy is informative — falls below n=200 after matched-budget routing is pre-declared INCONCLUSIVE for that benchmark's contribution to the ≥2-of-3 tally, never silently dropped and never scored as a null transfer gap.

**Expected outcome.** The probe retains matched-budget accuracy at or above the training-free self-consistency router at ≥3 of the 5 budget points on ≥2 of the 3 held-out benchmarks, with the cross-backbone (Llama-3.1) transfer gap larger than the cross-task transfer gap.

**Falsification condition.** Refuted if the probe drops below the training-free self-consistency router's matched-budget accuracy on held-out benchmarks at a majority of budget points — transfer so poor that a controller requiring no training at all dominates the trained probe, undermining the "train once, allocate cheaply" premise. In that case the deployable recipe is characterized as per-backbone probe retraining on recycled samples (cost quantified), not a transferable probe.

**Hypothesis (plain language).** A frozen probe's routing advantage shrinks under distribution shift but does not collapse to below a no-training floor, at least across held-out tasks; cross-backbone shift is the harder case.

**Metrics.** Matched-budget accuracy per held-out benchmark/backbone; transfer-gap estimate and 95% CI relative to the training-free router.

#### RQ5: Is the dual-self-report-variant safeguard load-bearing, or would a single variant have given the same verdict?

**Setup.** Recompute RQ2's non-inferiority/superiority verdict twice — once using only the verbalized-confidence variant as the self-report baseline, once using only the P(True)-style variant — and compare each single-variant verdict against the dual-variant (stronger-reported) verdict at all 5 pre-registered budget points on both benchmarks.

#### RQ6: Does a matched-budget win reflect genuine decision quality, or budget arithmetic alone?

**Setup.** Compute AUROC against cheap-tier correctness for the probe and the stronger self-report variant, with no cost accounting at all, on the same cached outputs used for RQ2; compare the sign of the AUROC gap against the sign of the matched-budget accuracy gap from RQ2 at each budget point.

**Expected outcome.** The probe's AUROC is not lower than self-report's AUROC, so that any matched-budget advantage found in RQ2 agrees in direction with the budget-independent decision-quality ranking. This direction is conceded as already evidenced in adjacent settings — probes out-score verbalized confidence as raw signals in Radharapu et al. (arXiv:2512.22245) and Yuan et al. (arXiv:2605.09502) — so RQ6's contribution is not the direction itself but its role as the isolation test that licenses interpreting RQ2's priced result.

**Falsification condition.** Refuted (mechanical-only win) if the probe wins the RQ2 matched-budget comparison at a given budget point while its AUROC is lower than self-report's at that same point — in which case the win is reported explicitly as an artifact of the probe's lower signal-acquisition cost, not evidence of better decisions.

**Hypothesis (plain language).** If the probe wins the headline comparison, it is because its decisions are genuinely better, not merely because it is cheaper to ask.

**Metrics.** AUROC(probe, cheap-tier correctness) vs. AUROC(self-report, cheap-tier correctness); sign agreement with the RQ2 matched-budget accuracy gap.

## Related Work

The closest prior work to this paper is DiffAdapt and AdaptiveThinking, and it is worth stating the delta plainly before surveying the wider landscape: both already train a lightweight probe on a frozen backbone's post-prefill hidden state to make a compute-allocation decision, but neither puts that probe head-to-head against large-model self-report, and neither reports a matched-dollar-budget frame in which every controller's own signal-acquisition cost is charged — both report token savings at preserved accuracy within one backbone's own reasoning modes, which is a different currency from the accuracy-per-dollar comparison this paper is built to run.

**Frozen-feature probing for compute allocation.** DiffAdapt trains an MLP on a frozen backbone's post-prefill final-layer hidden state to classify query difficulty into easy/normal/hard reasoning configurations; AdaptiveThinking trains a similar adapter on the same kind of prefill representation to predict a self-consistency score, deciding whether extended reasoning is warranted; KV-Embeddings pools cached key/value tensors instead of hidden states to reduce the storage cost of the same style of probe. All three establish that frozen-backbone representations carry usable difficulty signal, but none of them benchmarks that signal against self-report or training-free signals under one matched dollar budget — their comparison set is other hidden-state or trajectory-search methods, and their headline currency is token savings, not accuracy-per-dollar. Beyond compute allocation, the probe-versus-self-report contrast itself now has direct signal-quality evidence, and we concede it up front: Radharapu et al. (arXiv:2512.22245) show linear probes on a reasoning judge's hidden states are better calibrated than verbalized confidence and multi-generation methods at roughly a tenth of the compute, generalizing to unseen domains, and Yuan et al. (arXiv:2605.09502) find a linear probe predicts trace correctness at 0.95 AUROC while verbalized confidence on the very same wrong traces is nearly indistinguishable from confidence on correct ones. A probe out-scoring self-report on raw signal quality is therefore no longer a novel prediction — what neither work does is turn that signal into a budget-constrained routing decision: neither charges the probe's, the elicitation call's, or the sampling baseline's own acquisition cost against a shared dollar budget, and neither measures downstream routing accuracy at matched realized spend, which is precisely this paper's estimand.

**Self-report and its behavioral limits.** Kadavath et al. show that a large language model's own verbalized confidence and P(True)-style logprob correlate with correctness and are reasonably calibrated at scale, establishing self-report as a usable meta-decision signal — this paper adopts their P(True) elicitation format directly as one of the two self-report operationalizations metered below, not as a baseline whose own reported numbers are compared against; LimitedMetacognition finds, via an incentive-compatible delegate game, that models often fail to behaviorally deploy their own internal confidence when a decision actually depends on it, suggesting self-reports may be largely imitative rather than evidential; DoLarge (Barkan et al.) asks a closely related question — whether LLMs can predict their own task success in advance, and whether that self-assessment improves with experience — but studies in-advance capability self-assessment for multi-step agentic tasks rather than per-query routing between two fixed compute tiers, and does not compare self-assessment against an internal-state probe. None of the three prices the elicitation call itself or compares self-report against an internal-state probe as a budget-constrained controller — the gap this paper's dual-cost-metered router is built to close.

**Trained routing and cascades.** DiSRouter trains every model in a cost-ordered cascade, via SFT and then RL, to answer or explicitly forward a query to a stronger model, conceding in its own motivation that "intrinsic self-awareness is present but not sufficiently calibrated... for routing-quality decisions" without that training; TRIM routes at the reasoning-step level using a separately trained process-reward model to decide when a stronger model should regenerate a step; CaTS fine-tunes the base model itself to output calibrated one-pass confidence, replacing self-consistency sampling with a distilled signal. All three obtain a usable routing signal at the cost of nontrivial training investment — per-model, per-step, or backbone-modifying — that a frozen-probe controller is specifically built to avoid, since none of them evaluates a frozen-backbone alternative under the same budget.

**Router evaluation and adjacent probing objectives.** RouterArena provides an open evaluation platform for routers that choose between models, with metrics for accuracy, cost, and routing optimality; TPC extends linear probes into truncated polynomial classifiers for safety monitoring rather than correctness estimation; HaluNet trains a classifier on internal signals (log-likelihoods, entropies, hidden states) to score answer-level hallucination risk rather than difficulty-for-routing, but shares this paper's premise that a single generation's internal signals can substitute for an extra elicitation call. None of the three evaluates within-model compute-tier controllers, and none treats logit entropy, self-consistency, frozen probes, and self-report as interchangeable signals competing under one shared budget — the specific comparison this paper's harness is built to run.

**Compute-cost accounting as its own axis.** A separate line of work treats compute cost itself as a first-class evaluation axis without proposing a controller: Compute-Accuracy Pareto Frontiers maps the empirical accuracy/FLOPs frontier for open-source reasoning models; Cost Trade-offs of Reasoning and Non-Reasoning LLMs in Text-to-SQL shows that generation-time speed and cloud-billed execution cost can diverge, arguing token-count proxies can misprice a deployment's true cost; TimeBill frames inference as a hard wall-clock-budget-constrained optimization rather than a dollar-budget one; Efficient Multi-Model Orchestration routes prompts across models and backends by a weighted relevance/latency/cost score. Understanding and Steering the Cognitive Behaviors of Reasoning Models at Test-Time is adjacent in a different way: it identifies attention heads that predict and can be steered to change a model's reasoning trajectory (linear vs. non-linear), which is a mechanism for *changing* reasoning behavior at test time rather than a signal for *deciding* how much compute a query deserves. None of these five treats controller signal-acquisition cost itself as a quantity to be charged against the same budget a routing decision manages, which is this paper's specific accounting move.

## Conclusion

This work reframes a widespread but unpriced assumption — that a compute-allocation controller's own signal cost is negligible relative to the budget it manages — as a directly measurable question, rather than treating any one controller family as self-evidently the cheap or the good option. The central methodological wager is that "which controller wins" is only a meaningful claim once every controller's own signal-acquisition cost is charged against the same shared budget, once the self-report baseline is guarded against being an accidental strawman, and once a matched-budget win is checked against a budget-independent decision-quality signal so that cost artifacts and genuine signal-quality differences are not conflated. Whichever controller dominates for current backbones, the finding is decision-relevant: if the frozen probe is non-inferior to self-report, every deployed system currently paying for an extra elicitation call is paying for something a probe could deliver for free; if self-report retains an edge, that edge is now priced, telling a deployer exactly what its extra cost is buying and at which budgets. Two limitations bound this claim from the outset and are natural next steps rather than afterthoughts: the study is scoped to two-tier, within-model routing on exact-answer reasoning benchmarks, leaving model-choice routing across more than two backbones and agentic or tool-use workloads (where cost is dominated by environment interaction, not generation length) for follow-up; and the transfer research question (RQ4) may itself surface a transfer gap large enough to make per-backbone probe retraining, rather than a universally transferable probe, the practical deployment recipe — a finding this design is built to report honestly rather than average away.
