# Channel-Gated, Not Capability-Gated: Measuring Surplus Loss and Testing Constraint-as-Protection in LLM Bargaining

> Assembled from the six confirmed stage outputs in `pipeline.md`, post-audit (`taste_audit.md`: 🟢). Status: **specification / paper draft — no experiments have been run.** All numbers below are pre-registered design targets, not results.

## Abstract

Large language model agents are increasingly delegated bilateral negotiation on behalf of a principal, and a principal fielding a weaker or cheaper model may face a counterparty running a stronger one. How much bargaining surplus that weak agent loses as the capability gap widens — and whether a near-zero-cost defense exists — has not been measured. A recent wave of negotiation benchmarks now scores LLM-vs-LLM bargaining by continuous surplus against game-theoretic references [TermsBench2605; SupplyChainBargain2608; BilateralTradeEnv2604], but none manipulates a directed weak-vs-strong capability gap as a controlled gradient — the largest such study finds provider identity out-predicts capability rank for surplus capture, leaving the gap's own causal contribution open — while weak-vs-strong oversight studies score adversarial interaction only by win/loss outcomes or supervision-label quality, and existing findings that protocol-level restriction protects a weak principal always restrict the strong or untrusted side, never the weak party's own expressive channel. Our central claim, tested rather than assumed here, is that surplus extraction is gated by the action-space channel a bargaining protocol leaves open to the weaker party, not fixed by the capability gap alone — so narrowing the weak side's own outbound channel could in principle remove part of the surface through which a stronger counterparty's advantage converts into surplus, without changing either agent's underlying capability. We build a bilateral bargaining harness with a computable fair reference, quantify capability gap two ways, and measure how a weak agent's normalized surplus loss scales with that gap; we then delete the weak agent's free-text channel, leaving only schema-validated structured offers, and test — via a same-model causal contrast and a cross-tier substitution comparison — whether this restriction lowers surplus loss or lets a lower-capability tier match a higher one. A bilateral control and tactic-tagged transcripts attribute how much of any effect flows through rhetorical exploitation versus substantive bargaining strategy. We report the resulting scaling relationship and constraint-as-protection verdict, and what either implies for deploying weaker agents against unknown or stronger counterparties.

## Introduction

Autonomous LLM agents are increasingly delegated bilateral negotiation — price bargaining, resource splits, service contracts — on behalf of a human or organizational principal. A principal who deploys a cost-efficient, lower-capability model to save latency or spend has no guarantee that the counterparty on the other side of the table is running anything comparable; it may well be a considerably stronger model. This asymmetry is easy to create by accident (different vendors choose different tiers) and easy to create on purpose (a sophisticated counterparty has every incentive to field the strongest negotiator it can afford), and it raises a question that current deployment practice has no answer to.

Yet how much bargaining surplus a weak agent loses to a stronger adversary as the capability gap between them widens remains unmeasured, and so does whether anything short of matching capability can defend against it. Adjacent literatures have measured pieces of this question without answering it. A recent wave of negotiation-measurement work scores LLM-vs-LLM bargaining by continuous surplus against computable game-theoretic references [TermsBench2605; SupplyChainBargain2608; BilateralTradeEnv2604] — but treats model capability as an incidental attribute of whichever agents are plugged in, not as a manipulated, directed gradient; indeed, the largest cross-model study reports that provider identity predicts surplus capture better than capability rank [SupplyChainBargain2608], which makes the causal contribution of the gap itself an open, confounded question rather than a settled one. Work on scalable oversight measures weak-vs-strong adversarial interaction, but only through win/loss outcomes in oversight games or through how much supervision quality is recovered when a weak model trains a stronger one — never through a continuous economic outcome like a bargaining split scored against a computable reference. Work on AI control shows that restricting a party's protocol-level channel can protect a principal from an untrusted counterparty, but the restriction always falls on the strong, untrusted side or on the surrounding audit protocol; no work asks whether restricting the weak party's own channel protects it instead. The result is a genuine, unaddressed gap: a principal cannot currently set a safe operating threshold for how large a capability gap is tolerable, nor know whether mandating a cheap self-restriction on their own agent would help.

The nearest existing work approaches this gap from two different angles, and neither closes it alone. On the phenomenon side, research on AI-control protocols [AIControl] establishes that restricting a party's expressive channel can protect a principal against a stronger or untrusted counterparty — but always by restricting the strong, untrusted side or the surrounding audit protocol, never the weak party's own channel as self-protection, and never in a bargaining setting with a surplus split to score. On the method side, scalable-oversight research [ScalingLawsOversight] shows that weak-vs-strong adversarial outcomes scale predictably with capability gap and can be fit with a scaling law derived from round-robin play — but the outcome measured is oversight win-rate, not a continuous economic quantity, so the technique transfers cleanly while its result does not. And on the outcome side, the recent negotiation-measurement wave supplies exactly the continuous surplus-vs-reference machinery this paper needs [TermsBench2605; BilateralTradeEnv2604] — one harness even separates binding offers from free-text messages, though only as evaluation plumbing — but never manipulates the capability gap or the weak side's channel as treatments. Between these neighbors sits exactly the combination this paper needs: a controlled capability gradient, a continuous surplus-loss outcome, and a test of self-restriction on the weak side specifically.

Turning this gap into a measurable question surfaces its own difficulties. Capability is not a single scalar, so any "gap" between two models is a projection that different measures — a general-purpose benchmark delta, or a negotiation-specific skill rating fit from play — can disagree on, and two same-tier models can behave differently for reasons unrelated to any capability difference at all. Scoring a bargaining outcome as "lost surplus" requires a game complex enough to be non-trivial for LLM agents yet simple enough to admit a closed-form fair-reference split, and even a computable reference is itself a construct choice among several defensible candidates. Restricting the weak agent's action space removes both its exposure to rhetorical exploitation and its own legitimate self-defense moves, so whether restriction helps, hurts, or does nothing is not decidable in advance — it is the central, deliberately counter-intuitive question this paper poses. And even if restriction is shown to change the outcome, a transcript alone cannot say whether the change came from removing a rhetorical channel or from something else, since a stronger model plausibly out-negotiates on substance and out-persuades on rhetoric in the same turn.

Our approach turns each of these obstacles into a specific design choice. Capability gap is quantified two ways, and every scaling claim is required to hold under both measures at once, so disagreement between them becomes a reported finding about the gap's own structure rather than a hidden modeling choice. Every bargaining instance is scored against three independently computable fair-reference splits, and a zero-capability-gap self-play condition validates that the metric itself reads near zero when there is no gap to explain a loss — an existence check no prior weak-vs-strong study needs, because none of them scores a continuous surplus. The weak agent's action space is then restricted in the most minimal way possible — one field of its turn, the free-text message, is deleted, leaving only a schema-validated structured offer — which is what makes any observed effect attributable to channel removal specifically, tested through both a same-model contrast and a comparison against an unconstrained agent one capability tier higher. And a bilateral control, together with tactic-tagged transcripts, separates how much of any effect is rhetorical from how much is substantive bargaining skill.

We evaluate this design across bilateral multi-issue bargaining scenarios drawn from two established game families and a synthetic suite with controlled valuation asymmetry, comparing a capability ladder of LLM agents under both a free-text and a structured-offer condition for the weak side, against zero-gap self-play controls, a scripted non-adaptive opponent, and a bilateral both-constrained control.

In summary, we contribute:
- A formalization of channel-gated surplus extraction as this paper's central, falsifiable hypothesis in LLM bargaining — that how much of a capability gap converts into lost surplus is a function of the action-space channel a protocol leaves open to the weaker party, not of the capability gap alone — tested, not assumed, via the pre-registered contrasts below.
- A capability-gap-controlled bargaining harness with a dual gap measure and three independently computable fair-reference splits, including a zero-gap existence check that validates the surplus-loss metric before any causal claim is made on top of it.
- A minimal, single-field action-space restriction on the weak agent, tested through both a same-model causal contrast and a cross-tier substitution comparison, isolating whether restriction can protect a weak agent and whether it can substitute for capability.
- A channel-attribution analysis, combining a between-condition slope comparison with tactic-tagged transcripts, that separates rhetorical exploitation from substantive bargaining skill as the source of any observed effect.

## Problem Definition

**Formal setup.** Consider a directed model pair (w, s), where w is a weak-capability agent representing a principal and s is a strong-capability adversary. The pair negotiates over bargaining game instances g, each with private valuations, reservation values r_w and r_s known to each side but not its counterpart, and a deadline of T rounds. Each episode e under instance g and action-space condition c ∈ {structured, free} — where c is applied to w's turn only, except in a bilateral control where it is applied to both sides — yields an outcome consisting of an agreement flag and, if reached, a realized surplus split. One consequence of the unilateral design is stated here explicitly because the mechanism claims depend on it: under c = structured, only w's outbound message field is removed; s's free-text channel stays open and its messages remain visible to w, so the unilateral restriction closes the weak side's *outbound* expressive surface (its own leakage, engagement, and exploitable commitments) while leaving its *inbound* exposure to the adversary's rhetoric intact — the bilateral control is the condition that closes both surfaces. Normalized surplus loss for the weak side is L = (u_ref − u_realized) / (u_ref − r_w), with impasse scored at u_realized = r_w (so L = 1). The capability gap between w and s is quantified two ways for every directed pair: an exogenous gap Δ_a, a delta in a public general-capability score fixed before any bargaining occurs, and an endogenous gap Δ_b, a negotiation-specific skill rating fit from round-robin free-text self-play. The object of study is how L scales with Δ (under both Δ_a and Δ_b) in each action-space condition, and whether E[L | c = structured] is lower than E[L | c = free] at matched Δ for the same pair.

**Why this is hard.** Fixing a game instance g does not fix a unique value for u_ref: an even split of the disposable surplus, the Nash bargaining solution given each side's reservation value, and the Kalai–Smorodinsky point are all independently defensible candidates, and they can disagree on both magnitude and, in asymmetric instances, direction — so any surplus-loss reading is only as meaningful as the reference it is computed against. The capability gap Δ is similarly not observationally unique: Δ_a and Δ_b can order the same model pairs differently, and functional similarity between two same-tier models can modulate outcomes independent of any capability difference at all, so a scaling claim that ignores this risks attributing a pair-similarity effect to capability itself. Restricting c from free to structured removes an entire message field from w's turn, and because a stronger adversary can extract surplus through better substantive play, through rhetorical tactics in free text, or through both simultaneously in the same turn, the resulting change in L cannot be attributed to one channel over the other from the outcome alone — a claim about *why* restriction works (if it works) requires evidence beyond the aggregate loss comparison itself. Finally, the outcome of restricting c is not decidable from first principles: removing w's free-text field removes both its exposure to persuasive exploitation and its own legitimate self-defense moves (framing, stalling, commitment language), so a design that only measures whether L goes down cannot rule out that it goes down for the wrong reason, or goes up.

## Method Overview

At a conceptual level, the study is a single measurement pipeline with four cooperating parts. Bargaining instances and a capability ladder of agent pairs enter the pipeline through a harness that computes a fair-reference split for every instance and validates itself against a zero-gap null; a dual capability-gap measure turns each directed pair into two candidate scalars, against which the resulting surplus-loss series is regressed to obtain a scaling estimate; a minimal action-space restriction is then applied to the weak agent specifically, and the same harness re-scores outcomes under both the restricted and unrestricted condition to obtain a same-model contrast and a cross-tier substitution comparison; and a channel-attribution analysis, combining the between-condition slope comparison with tactic-tagged transcripts of the adversary's free-text messages, apportions any observed effect between rhetorical and substantive causes.

The **dual capability-gap quantification** module assigns two candidate gap scalars to every directed model pair and requires scaling claims to hold under both, reporting disagreement as a finding about the gap's own structure rather than resolving it by fiat. The **bargaining harness with computable reference splits** module instantiates game instances with a closed-form Pareto frontier and three fair-reference points, and validates the resulting surplus-loss metric against a zero-capability-gap self-play condition before any gap-dependent claim is trusted. The **structured-offer constraint** module deletes the weak agent's free-text field, leaving one schema-validated offer object per turn, and compares outcomes under this restriction both against the same model unrestricted and against an unrestricted model one capability tier higher. The **channel attribution** module apportions any observed restriction effect between the rhetorical and substantive channels, using a between-condition slope comparison as a coarse causal bound and tactic-tagged transcripts as a finer, correlational account.

## Methodology

### Dual Capability-Gap Quantification

**Challenge.** Capability is not a single scalar, so any gap between two models is a projection, and different projections can disagree on both the magnitude and the ordering of the same model pairs — while pair-level functional similarity can modulate outcomes independent of any capability difference at all.

**Insight.** The ambiguity cannot be resolved by picking the "correct" gap measure, but it can be turned into an explicit, checkable robustness condition rather than a hidden modeling choice.

**Method.** Every directed model pair is assigned two gap measures: an exogenous delta in a public general-capability score, fixed before any bargaining occurs, and an endogenous negotiation-specific skill rating fit from round-robin free-text self-play, following the role-specific rating-estimation technique used in prior weak-vs-strong oversight-scaling work. The endogenous rating is fit on a pre-registered, disjoint set of rating-estimation episodes: none of the episodes used to fit Δ_b enters any loss regression downstream, so the regressor is never a function of its own outcome sample. Normalized surplus loss is regressed on each gap measure independently via a mixed-effects model with random intercepts for game instance and model pair; a scaling claim is reported only when both measures agree in sign, and disagreement is reported explicitly as evidence of a projection-dependent gap axis.

### Bargaining Harness with Computable Reference Splits

**Challenge.** Scoring an agreed split as "lost surplus" requires a bargaining environment complex enough to be non-trivial for LLM agents yet simple enough to admit a closed-form fair reference for every instance — and even a computable reference is itself a construct choice among several defensible candidates.

**Insight.** A surplus-loss metric that has not been validated against its own null condition cannot be trusted at any nonzero gap either; if the metric reads a large loss when there is no capability gap to explain it, the game or the reference choice — not the phenomenon — is what is broken.

**Method.** Bargaining instances are drawn from parameterized game families with known private valuations, reservation values, and a computable Pareto frontier, over which three fair-reference points are computed for every instance: an even split, the Nash bargaining solution, and the Kalai–Smorodinsky point. Zero-capability-gap self-play episodes (weak-vs-weak and strong-vs-strong) provide an existence check: if mean normalized surplus loss under self-play is not close to zero under the primary reference, the harness itself — not the capability-gap hypothesis — is treated as the thing to fix before any scaling claim is trusted.

### Structured-Offer Constraint

**Challenge.** Restricting the weak agent's action space removes both its exposure to rhetorical exploitation and its own legitimate self-defense moves, so whether restriction protects the weak agent, harms it, or does nothing is not decidable from first principles.

**Insight.** A minimal, single-field deletion — removing exactly the weak agent's free-text message and nothing else — is what makes any observed effect attributable to channel removal specifically, rather than to a more elaborate redesign whose effect could come from anywhere.

**Method.** In the constrained condition, the weak agent's entire turn is one schema-validated object specifying its action and offered allocation, with no natural-language field transmitted and a bounded number of resamples on parse failure; the free-text condition is identical except for an additional unrestricted message field. The same model pair and game instance are compared under both conditions at matched capability gap (a same-model contrast), and a constrained weak agent at one capability tier is separately compared against an unconstrained weak agent at the next tier against the same adversary (a cross-tier substitution comparison) — testing not only whether restriction helps, but whether it can stand in for a tier of capability. Because the restriction is unilateral, what it deletes is precisely the weak agent's outbound expressive surface; any protective effect it shows therefore operates through what w can no longer say (leakage, engagement, exploitable commitments), not through shielding w from what s still says — the inbound surface is closed only in the bilateral control.

### Channel Attribution

**Challenge.** A stronger adversary can extract surplus through better substantive bargaining or through rhetorical tactics in free text, and these two channels are not separable from the outcome alone, since both can operate in the same turn.

**Insight.** A between-condition comparison gives a coarse but causal bound on how much of an effect is rhetorical, while an independent, tactic-level reading of the free-text channel gives a finer-grained but correlational account of which specific tactics carry that effect — keeping the two readings at their true, different evidence strengths rather than conflating them into one number.

**Method.** The reduction in the capability-gap-to-surplus-loss slope between the free-text and structured conditions is computed as a causal bound on the rhetorical channel's contribution. Independently, an LLM judge tags each adversary free-text message against a fixed tactic taxonomy (anchoring, misrepresentation of value, false deadline or commitment, flattery or pressure), with a subset human-double-coded to validate judge reliability, and tactic incidence is related to per-episode surplus loss. A bilateral control, in which both sides are restricted to structured offers, checks whether any residual gap-dependent loss in the structured condition is substantive rather than rhetorical.

## Experiments

### Experimental Setup

**Datasets.** GLEE bargaining game configurations [GLEE2410] — a parameterized economic-game benchmark used as the primary game family because its parameterization gives direct, controlled sweeps over surplus size and valuation asymmetry, letting the harness be built and validated cheaply before scaling up. Deal-or-No-Deal multi-issue division scenarios [DealOrNoDeal1706] — real multi-issue value functions with private per-item valuations, used to test whether the surplus-loss scaling relationship generalizes beyond GLEE's parameterized configurations to a second, independently designed game family. A synthetic three-issue divide-the-pie suite (generated) — lets valuation asymmetry be swept as a controlled, continuous parameter independent of whichever asymmetry distribution the two fixed corpora happen to contain, specifically to test whether gap-measure disagreement, should it occur, concentrates at high-asymmetry instances as a designed sweep rather than only being observed post hoc.

**Baselines** (sanity-check comparators, not competitors to beat — this is a measurement study, and no research question here is framed as "our procedure beats baseline X"). Zero-capability-gap self-play (weak-vs-weak and strong-vs-strong) is the necessary existence check for every gap-dependent claim. A scripted, non-adaptive Boulware or linear-conceder opponent — memoryless and capability-independent by construction — checks that an observed gap-loss trend reflects the strong adversary's capability specifically, not generic concession-strength differences against any sufficiently persistent opponent. A bilateral both-constrained control cell, in which the adversary is also restricted to structured offers, isolates whether any residual structured-condition effect is substantive rather than rhetorical.

**Metrics.** Normalized surplus loss and its mixed-effects trend slope under each capability-gap measure; agreement and impasse rates; Pareto-efficiency of realized splits (distance to the frontier); the condition main effect and condition-by-gap interaction from the constraint contrast; adversary tactic incidence and its association with per-episode loss.

**Thin-cell rule.** Any gap-level × condition × game-family cell with fewer than 30 valid episodes (below the powered n = 50 target) is pre-declared inconclusive for that cell: its data are reported but excluded from the primary tests above, and never scored as a null result or dropped silently. If more than a quarter of the cells feeding a central test (RQ2's pooled slope, or RQ3's pooled condition effect) are thin, that whole test is reported as inconclusive rather than as a null finding.

**Scale and budget (design targets).** The full grid implies roughly 15,000–18,000 episodes: the RQ2 scale-up (36 directed pairs and self-play controls × 3 game families × 50 episodes ≈ 5,400 free-text episodes), its structured-condition mirror for RQ3 (≈ 5,400), the bilateral both-constrained control at three gap levels (≈ 4,500), and scripted-opponent, pilot, rating-estimation, and judge-calibration cells (≈ 2,500). At 3k–15k tokens per multi-turn episode this is an order-of-magnitude token budget of 50–270M tokens, dominated by the strong-side model's turns. These figures are stated as auditable pre-registration targets — they are what the thin-cell rule is checked against — not as results.

**Research questions:**
1. RQ1 — Does the surplus-loss metric read near zero when there is no capability gap to explain a loss?
2. RQ2 — Does normalized surplus loss scale with capability gap, and do the two gap measures agree?
3. RQ3 — Does restricting the weak agent to structured offers lower its surplus loss relative to free text, at matched capability gap?
4. RQ4 — Can a structured-offer restriction let a lower-capability tier match a higher one against the same adversary?
5. RQ5 — How much of any restriction effect is rhetorical versus substantive?

#### RQ1: Does the surplus-loss metric read near zero when there is no capability gap to explain a loss?

**Setup.** Zero-capability-gap self-play episodes (weak-vs-weak and strong-vs-strong) are run across all tested game families under the free-text condition and scored under the primary (Nash-bargaining) reference. Sample size: sized to estimate the self-play mean surplus loss to within ±0.02 units at 95% confidence.

**Expected outcome.** Mean normalized surplus loss under self-play is close to zero: |E[L | Δ = 0]| ≤ 0.05 under the primary reference.

**Falsification condition.** Refuted if |E[L | Δ = 0]| exceeds 0.05 — this would indicate the harness itself (the game design or the reference-split computation), rather than the capability gap, is producing an apparent loss, and every downstream gap-dependent claim in RQ2–RQ5 would need to be revisited before being trusted.

**Hypothesis (plain language).** The measurement instrument reads a near-null result when there is genuinely nothing for the capability gap to explain, which is a precondition for trusting any nonzero reading elsewhere.

**Metrics.** Mean and 95% confidence interval of normalized surplus loss under self-play, per game family and reference split.

#### RQ2: Does normalized surplus loss scale with capability gap, and do the two gap measures agree?

**Setup.** Pilot stage: a capability ladder spanning several tiers against a fixed strong adversary, free-text condition, one game family. Scale-up: a full round-robin over the capability ladder (directed unequal pairs plus zero-gap self-play controls) across all three game families. Mixed-effects model: surplus loss ~ gap + (1 | game instance) + (1 | model pair), two-sided Wald test on the gap coefficient, α = 0.05, computed separately for the exogenous and endogenous gap measures. Rating-estimation episodes for the endogenous measure are disjoint from the measurement episodes entering this regression (see Methodology), so Δ_b is fixed before the regression rather than estimated from its own outcome data.

**Sample-size rationale.** Per-cell episode counts are sized to detect a standardized gap-slope effect of d ≈ 0.4 at approximately 80% power; an observed effect below that in the pilot triggers a pre-committed enlargement of the scale-up sample, not a null claim.

**Expected outcome.** Normalized surplus loss increases with capability gap under both measures (significantly positive Wald test, p < 0.05, same sign for both), with a monotone trend across gap levels (Spearman ρ > 0), and the effect clears a pre-registered practical-significance floor: the model-implied increase in loss from the smallest to the largest tested gap level (slope × range of the gap) is at least 0.10 normalized-loss units under each gap measure.

**Falsification condition.** Refuted toward "no scaling relationship" if the gap coefficient is not significantly positive under either measure, or the trend is flat or non-monotone (Spearman ρ ≤ 0); refuted toward "gap is projection-dependent" (a distinct, also-reportable outcome, not treated as a null result) if the two gap measures yield significantly opposite-signed coefficients; and a slope that is statistically significant but implies less than 0.10 normalized-loss units of change across the tested gap range is reported as "detected but below the pre-registered practical-significance floor," not as a passing scaling relationship — significance without magnitude is not sufficient here.

**Hypothesis (plain language).** A weak agent's surplus loss against a stronger adversary grows as the capability gap widens, and this growth is not an artifact of which gap measure is used.

**Metrics.** Per-measure mixed-effects slope and 95% confidence interval; Spearman correlation of loss with gap level; sign-agreement between the two gap measures.

#### RQ3: Does restricting the weak agent to structured offers lower its surplus loss relative to free text, at matched capability gap?

**Setup.** For each tested model pair and gap level, the same pair negotiates matched game instances under both the free-text and structured-offer condition for the weak side. A two-condition mixed-effects factorial estimates the condition main effect and the condition-by-gap interaction, with Holm correction across the two primary tests, α = 0.05.

**Expected outcome.** The condition main effect is negative — normalized surplus loss is lower under the structured-offer condition than under free text at matched gap, with a point-estimate reduction of at least 0.05 normalized-loss units — and the condition-by-gap interaction is positive, indicating the reduction grows as the gap widens.

**Falsification condition.** Refuted if the condition main effect is non-significant or non-negative under Holm-corrected α = 0.05, or if the point-estimate reduction falls below 0.05 units — either would mean expressive freedom does not expose the weak agent to additional loss, and restriction is neutral-to-harmful rather than protective.

**Hypothesis (plain language).** Deleting the weak agent's free-text channel reduces the surplus it loses to a stronger adversary at the same capability gap, and the protective effect is larger when the gap is larger.

**Note on interpreting the zero-gap cells.** A controlled human-subjects study using a near-identical structured-offer-vs-free-text manipulation between symmetric-capability dyads found the average agreed split statistically unchanged between conditions (see Related Work) — so a near-zero condition main effect specifically within this paper's own zero-gap self-play cells is the pattern that prior result predicts, not evidence against this hypothesis. The diagnostic test for this research question is the condition-by-gap interaction, not the marginal condition effect at Δ ≈ 0.

**Metrics.** Condition main-effect coefficient and 95% confidence interval; condition-by-gap interaction coefficient and confidence interval.

#### RQ4: Can a structured-offer restriction let a lower-capability tier match a higher one against the same adversary?

**Setup.** For each adjacent pair of capability tiers (t, t+1), a constrained weak agent at tier t is compared against an unconstrained weak agent at tier t+1, both facing the same adversary. A one-sided mixed-effects non-inferiority test (α = 0.05, Holm-corrected across tested adjacent pairs) evaluates whether the tier-t constrained agent's surplus loss is no greater than the tier-(t+1) unconstrained agent's *plus a pre-registered non-inferiority margin δ = 0.05 normalized-loss units* — i.e., whether L(t, structured) ≤ L(t+1, free) + 0.05. The explicit margin matters: "no greater than" alone, with no stated δ, would silently be a superiority test mislabeled as a non-inferiority one.

**Expected outcome.** For at least one adjacent tier pair, the constrained lower-tier agent's surplus loss is within the pre-registered δ = 0.05 margin of the unconstrained higher-tier agent's — restriction substitutes for at least one tier of capability.

**Falsification condition.** Refuted if no adjacent tier pair satisfies L(t, structured) ≤ L(t+1, free) + 0.05 at Holm-corrected α = 0.05 — this would mean restriction, even where protective in the same-model contrast (RQ3), cannot compensate for a genuine capability-tier gap even within a 0.05-unit margin, and capability remains the dominant lever.

**Hypothesis (plain language).** Restricting a weaker model's action space can, in at least some cases, substitute for giving it more capability.

**Metrics.** Cross-tier mean surplus-loss difference and one-sided confidence bound, per adjacent tier pair.

#### RQ5: How much of any restriction effect is rhetorical versus substantive?

**Setup.** The reduction in the mixed-effects gap-loss slope between the free-text and structured conditions is computed as a between-condition slope-removal ratio. Because the unilateral structured condition closes only the weak agent's outbound surface while the bilateral control also closes the adversary's free-text channel, the free/structured/bilateral cell triple separates the two rhetorical surfaces: the free-vs-structured slope difference bounds the outbound-and-engagement component, and the structured-vs-bilateral difference bounds the residual inbound-persuasion component. Independently, adversary free-text messages are tagged against a fixed tactic taxonomy by an LLM judge, with a subset human-double-coded to establish judge reliability, and tactic incidence is related to per-episode surplus loss via a count-outcome regression. The bilateral both-constrained control provides an additional check on whether residual structured-condition loss is substantive.

**Expected outcome.** The slope-removal ratio exceeds one-half (the structured condition removes more than half of the free-text gap slope), and adversary tactic incidence correlates positively with per-episode surplus loss (p < 0.05).

**Falsification condition.** Refuted (exploitation is substantive, not rhetorical) if the slope-removal ratio is near zero — i.e., the structured condition's gap slope is nearly as steep as free text's — regardless of whether tactic incidence correlates with loss in the free-text condition alone; this would mean any protective effect found in RQ3 works through decision-simplification rather than channel closure, and would revise the mechanism account behind restriction-as-protection even if RQ3 and RQ4 hold.

**Hypothesis (plain language, exploratory — not a confirmatory claim).** Whatever surplus a stronger adversary extracts from a wider capability gap flows predominantly through rhetorical tactics in free text, not through better substantive offer strategy.

**Metrics.** Slope-removal ratio with bootstrap confidence interval; tactic incidence rate per transcript; regression coefficient of loss on tactic count.

## Related Work

The nearest prior work to this paper splits across three lines, and none closes the gap alone. On the phenomenon side, research on AI-control protocols [AIControl] establishes that restricting a party's expressive channel can protect a principal against a stronger or untrusted counterparty — but always by restricting the strong, untrusted side or the surrounding audit protocol, never the weak party's own channel as self-protection, and never in a bargaining setting with a surplus split to score. On the method side, scalable-oversight research [ScalingLawsOversight] shows that weak-vs-strong adversarial outcomes scale predictably with capability gap via a scaling law fit from round-robin play — but its outcome is oversight win-rate, not a continuous economic quantity, so its technique transfers cleanly to this paper's endogenous gap measure while its own findings do not. Closest of all on the manipulated variable itself, a controlled human-subjects bargaining experiment [LanguageOfBargaining2023] contrasts an alternating structured-offer condition against unrestricted natural-language bargaining and finds the average agreed split statistically unchanged between conditions, though the natural-language condition reaches agreement faster and with lower variance — a near-identical intervention to this paper's own structured-offer constraint, conceded here as a rival prediction for this paper's own zero-gap placebo (symmetric capability should show no split-level effect either), but run on symmetric-capability human dyads with no reference-split/surplus-loss framing and no adversarial-exploitation question, so it cannot speak to whether the same constraint protects a weak party once a capability gap opens up. Between these three neighbors sits exactly the combination this paper is built to test: a controlled capability gradient, a continuous surplus-loss outcome, and a self-restriction test on the weak side specifically.

**Weak-vs-strong capability-gap measurement.** Scalable-oversight work [ScalingLawsOversight] models mismatched adversarial interaction as two-player games and fits a scaling law linking general capability to role-specific outcome ratings; weak-to-strong generalization work [WeakToStrong] manipulates a capability gap as a controlled variable in a finetuning pipeline and introduces a metric for how much of that gap is recovered. Separately, work on judge–model functional similarity [GreatModelsThinkAlike] shows that weak-to-strong outcomes are modulated by how similar a model pair is, independent of any capability difference between them. All three establish that capability-gap-dependent outcomes are real, measurable, and potentially confounded by pair similarity — but none scores a continuous economic outcome such as bargaining surplus against a computable reference, and none is set in an adversarial bargaining interaction at all; the bargaining-side complement — continuous surplus without a manipulated gap — is the measurement wave covered next.

**Continuous-surplus negotiation measurement.** A recent wave of benchmarks supplies the outcome machinery this paper builds on, without the treatment axes it needs. TERMS-Bench [TermsBench2605] makes the environment itself the verifier in Bayesian negotiation games — the counterpart's private state is hidden from the agent but observable to the evaluator — yielding continuous optimality-gap and surplus diagnostics across thirteen LLM agents. A supply-chain bargaining study [SupplyChainBargain2608] benchmarks nine LLMs against a validated Perfect Bayesian Equilibrium across 9,840 LLM-to-LLM negotiations and finds that while capability governs value creation and contract reliability, provider identity predicts who captures surplus better than capability rank — concurrent work, and the single strongest reason the gap's causal contribution needs a design that manipulates it: this paper's dual gap measure and pair-level random effects exist precisely to separate gap from pair identity, the confound that study reports but cannot resolve. A bilateral-trade training environment [BilateralTradeEnv2604] runs a round-robin tournament of five frontier models over 15,000 negotiations with surplus-share outcomes, and its harness even separates binding offers from natural-language messages — but as evaluation plumbing, never as a manipulated treatment, and it observes only that weaker models survive on wide-ZOPA instances rather than fitting any loss-vs-gap relationship. None of the three manipulates a directed capability gradient, validates its surplus metric at zero gap, or tests restriction of the weak side's channel; all three leave this paper's estimand open while supplying its scoring machinery.

**Protocol-level restriction as protection.** Work on AI-control protocols [AIControl] evaluates safety procedures under the assumption that the most capable party is adversarial, and shows that restricting that party's channel — or the surrounding monitoring and audit protocol — can prevent catastrophic outcomes; related work [ControlTax] prices the overhead of such protective restrictions in resource terms. Both establish that protocol-level restriction can protect a weaker or trusted principal, but the restriction always falls on the strong, untrusted side; neither asks whether restricting the weak party's own channel offers comparable protection, and neither scores a continuous surplus split rather than a catastrophe probability. Closest on the defended party itself, EQ-Negotiator [EQNegotiator2511] protects a small model in adversarial credit negotiation by *adding* strategic scaffolding — a game-theoretic reasoning layer with HMM-tracked emotional personas — showing a weak agent can be defended without more raw capability; it is the additive complement of this paper's subtractive question (delete a channel rather than bolt on a reasoning system), and it neither varies the capability gap as a treatment nor scores surplus against a computable reference.

**Strategic communication and free-form action spaces.** Formal treatments of strategic persuasion [StrategicPersuasion] convert communication into sender-receiver games with explicit utilities, scoring how much a sender can shift a fixed receiver's action through language alone — operationalizing exactly the rhetorical channel a structured-offer constraint would delete, but only for a fixed receiver, never varying receiver capability relative to sender or testing a structural defense on the receiver's side. Evolutionary search over emotional-expression policies [EvoEmo2509] sharpens the same point empirically: optimized rhetorical and emotional pressure extracts measurably higher payoff from counterpart LLMs in multi-turn price negotiation — direct evidence that the rhetorical channel prices, though with no attribution of how much of a *capability gap's* effect flows through that channel, which is RQ5's estimand. Separately, work on training language agents for negotiation [ARIA] treats the exponential free-form action space as a training-time credit-assignment problem to be compressed for sample efficiency, rather than as a deployment-time exploitation surface, and never compares constrained against free-form action spaces under adversarial pressure between an already-capable pair. Evidence from a different exploitation surface reinforces the plausibility that an agent's own interaction channel — not just its raw capability — is what an adversary exploits: web agents are shown to be systematically manipulated by interface-level dark patterns [Decepticon2512], and a separate benchmark measures how often persuasive language redirects a web agent away from its intended task [TRAP2512]; both are domain-specific (web navigation, not bargaining) and outcome-specific (task redirection, not surplus split), but both support, without testing, the same channel-exploitability premise this paper's structured-offer constraint is built to test directly. The four-label adversary-tactic taxonomy used in the channel-attribution analysis is grounded in established negotiation-tactics inventories and in a formal vocabulary for adversarial dialogue acts from this same literature [BEDA2512], rather than invented ad hoc, and is piloted and confusion-checked against human raters before being frozen for scale.

**LLM-agent economic interaction and negotiation-dialogue foundations.** Persistent multi-agent competition frameworks [CompeteAI] study cumulative advantage and strategy adaptation among LLM agents in resource-constrained markets, but among agents of roughly homogeneous capability and without a closed-form reference split, so no per-interaction surplus loss can be attributed to capability-driven exploitation. The canonical multi-issue negotiation-dialogue task [DealOrNoDeal1706] and a parameterized suite of bargaining-game configurations [GLEE2410] each provide real game families and value-function designs this paper's harness draws on directly, but neither manipulates capability gap as a controlled independent variable or tests action-space restriction as a protective intervention — they evaluate whichever models happen to be plugged in, at whatever capability those models happen to have. Closest in relational structure of all, a concurrent line of work [PrincipalLoyalty2606] studies an almost identical three-role setup — a principal, an agent representing it, and an adversarial counterparty — and measures loyalty failures such as information leakage and capitulation under conversational pressure; it shares this paper's core principal/agent/adversary framing but has no capability-gap axis and no economic reference split, so it cannot say how much surplus is lost per unit of capability gap or whether restricting the agent's own channel helps or hurts.

## Conclusion

This work reframes a deployment assumption — that a weak principal's negotiating agent is simply at the mercy of whatever capability gap it happens to face — as a measurable, and partly addressable, question. The central methodological wager is that surplus loss under capability asymmetry is not fixed by the gap alone but gated by the action-space channel a bargaining protocol leaves open to the weaker party, and that this channel-gating claim is only trustworthy once the surplus-loss metric is validated at zero gap, the gap itself is checked under two independent measures, and any protective effect of restriction is tested both as a same-model contrast and as a cross-tier substitution, not asserted from a single comparison. Whichever way the constraint-as-protection tests resolve, the finding is decision-relevant: if restriction protects the weak agent and can substitute for a tier of capability, that is a near-zero-cost deployment mitigation available today; if it does not, capability itself remains the only lever, and principals need a different answer than "restrict the weak side's messages." Two limitations bound this claim from the outset and are natural next steps rather than afterthoughts: the design is scoped to sandboxable, two-party bargaining with computable valuations, leaving many-to-many markets with partner choice and non-sandboxable live deployments for follow-up; and the dual-gap-measure check (RQ2) may itself surface genuine disagreement between the two capability projections in high-asymmetry scenarios, which this design reports as a finding about the gap's own structure rather than resolving by picking one measure as authoritative — pointing toward a richer, multi-dimensional capability construct as the next methodological target.
