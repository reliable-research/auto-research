# Existence Is Attacker-Controlled: Provenance Gating Against Slopsquatting Across Package, MCP, and Skill Registries

## Abstract

LLM coding assistants and autonomous agents recommend packages, MCP servers, and agent skills, and increasingly install them with little human review. Because these systems hallucinate plausible-but-nonexistent names at stable, predictably recurrent rates, an adversary can pre-register a predicted name and convert a generation error into code execution — the *slopsquatting* attack. The defenses proposed for agent-facing registries gate trust on **existence-class predicates**: does the name resolve, is the artifact old enough, popular enough, unflagged. Our thesis is that these predicates fail *by construction, not by implementation*: the attacker controls **when** registration begins and therefore satisfies existence, age, and popularity on their own schedule. We make this quantitative rather than definitional. We build a fully simulated, no-proprietary-data harness that (1) seeds a squatting adversary from published name-recurrence statistics, (2) instantiates three registry substrates — npm/PyPI packages, MCP servers, and agent-skill marketplaces — from real public metadata, and (3) measures the security/usability operating point of each defense against a common install-opportunity denominator, across a hallucination-rate sweep from 4.6% to 43%. We contrast existence-class defenses (existence check, RAG-grounding at its published 40.8%→3.2% block / ~16% recall point, self-audit at 51–53%, name reservation) with a **trust-tiered install-time provenance gate** (untrusted / signed / attested-publisher / reputation-established) whose acceptance the attacker cannot satisfy without compromising a trusted identity. The paper's central artifact is a registration-cost-per-predicate-class table showing every existence predicate is cheaply and predictably satisfiable, and an operating-point frontier on which the provenance gate dominates the existence-class cluster. We report the price-swept lineage-acquisition sensitivity (buying an aged, qualified identity), the provenance gate's own failure modes, and four printed falsification conditions.

## 1. Introduction — the stance

Large language models now participate directly in software-supply-chain decisions: they generate imports, recommend dependencies, name MCP servers, and issue installation commands, and agent frameworks increasingly act on those outputs without a human in the loop. This practice extends past npm and PyPI to MCP registries and agent-skill marketplaces that are being stood up now with existence-only or no gating. LLMs, however, recurrently emit plausible names for artifacts that do not exist. *Skills That Don't Exist* (arXiv:2607.12340) measures hallucinated-skill recommendation at 36.0/36.9/43.1% across settings, 5,669 distinct hallucinated names, self-audit only 51–53% accurate, and — critically — **predictable recurrence**: the same fabricated names reappear across independent runs. A follow-on frontier-cohort study (Churilov, arXiv:2605.17062) reports the rate compressing to 4.6–6.1% on 2026 models while the recurrent, registrable structure persists. Recurrence is exactly the quantity an attacker needs to pre-register, and the quantity a realistic simulation needs to seed an adversary.

The defense literature answers this threat with **existence-class predicates**. Registry operators and agent frameworks check that a name resolves, that a package is old enough or popular enough, or that it is not flagged; the slopsquatting systematic review endorses *existence validation*, and 2607.12340 itself closes with name reservation and RAG-grounding. Every one of these predicates is a property of a *registered artifact*. Our thesis is that this is the wrong place to put the trust decision:

> **Existence is attacker-satisfiable by construction; provenance is not.** An adversary who predicts a name and registers it early makes existence, age, and popularity true *on their own schedule*. Grounding a name against a real index does not help — the grounded name the attacker registered *is* real. Only a predicate the attacker cannot satisfy without compromising a **trusted publisher identity** — cryptographic provenance — moves the trust decision off the attacker's clock.

A reviewer will object that "a registered name exists" is a tautology. Granted — and that is precisely why the contribution is not the truism but its **quantified economics and operating-point comparison**. We ask: how cheaply, and with what lead time, can an attacker satisfy each *graded* existence predicate (not just existence, but age ≥ N days, downloads ≥ K, not-flagged) given the published recurrence distribution; and what security/usability point does a trust-tiered provenance gate reach versus the existence-class defenses on the *same* simulated attack. History-dependent gates (age, provenance-linkage, maintainer history) are interesting because they convert an attacker **timing** problem into a **resource-acquisition** problem: when the lead time from prediction to victim installation is short, no fresh registration matures in time, so the only route is to *acquire an already-qualified identity* — which costs cash and, for provenance, requires compromising a trusted publisher. Whether a predicate is attacker-satisfiable is therefore a question of *reachability under a budget*, and that is what we certify.

**Contributions.**
- **C1.** A literature-seeded slopsquatting-race simulator: an adversary drawn from published name-recurrence statistics, three registry substrates (npm/PyPI, MCP servers, agent-skill marketplaces) built from real public metadata, and a common install-opportunity ledger that aligns attacker probes, recommendations, registry events, and installs on one denominator. No proprietary or trust-annotated data is required.
- **C2.** A registration-cost-per-predicate-class analysis proving each existence predicate (existence, age, popularity, not-flagged, RAG-grounded) is cheaply and predictably satisfiable, with attacker cost taken from real registry mechanics and a price-swept sensitivity for the identity-acquisition route.
- **C3.** A trust-tiered install-time provenance gate (untrusted / signed / attested-publisher / reputation-established) with measured security/usability operating points, evaluated head-to-head against four published existence-class baselines at their published strength.
- **C4.** The three-registry-class generalization: the existence-fails / provenance-holds *structure* recurs across packages, MCP servers, and skills, reported as per-class operating points rather than one pooled threshold.

## 2. Background and related work

**Package hallucination measurement.** Spracklen et al. (*We Have a Package for You!*, USENIX Security 2025) quantify hallucination prevalence and compare generation-time mitigations (RAG, self-refinement, fine-tuning). Krishna et al. (*Importing Phantoms*) characterize rates across models/languages/tasks against time-indexed registry records. Churilov (*The Range Shrinks, the Threat Remains*, arXiv:2605.17062) replicates on 2026 frontier models and isolates universally-hallucinated, registrable names. Zhao et al. (HFUZZER) expose hallucinations by phrase-based fuzzing. These establish the attack surface and the recurrence structure we seed from; none evaluates an install-time gate against an adversary who chooses registration timing.

**The anchor and method twin.** *Skills That Don't Exist* (arXiv:2607.12340) supplies our adversary's name distribution and three baseline operating points, and closes by recommending name reservation and RAG-grounding — both existence-class — as future install-time defenses. We take up exactly that future work and show the recommended reservation is a race the defender loses whenever the attacker predicts and registers first, which the paper's own recurrence statistics make feasible.

**Registry-based detection and provenance — the concede-and-claim.** ConfuGuard (Jiang, Çakar, Lysenko & Davis, arXiv:2502.20528) detects package-confusion via name embeddings + metadata + popularity-based trust; Hillah et al. (arXiv:2606.13918) calibrate per-import hallucination probability from PyPI metadata; both rely on fixed registry evidence and resemblance to established packages, so they do not tell us which signals survive attacker-controlled timing. On provenance, McCann (*Cryptographic Registry Provenance*, arXiv:2605.03309) binds publisher/registry identity to artifacts via signatures and install-time verification, and the **Trustworthy MCP Registry** (Future Internet 18(5):243, doi 10.3390/fi18050243) specifies a Sigstore-based signing/attestation architecture for MCP servers. *We concede the mechanism*: we do not invent cryptographic provenance and we cite these as the reference "how." *Our delta* is (a) the defeated-by-construction demonstration that makes provenance non-optional rather than merely advisable, (b) the trust-tiering **policy** and its measured operating points — a graded gate with an explicit security/usability curve that a single-tier MCP-only Sigstore gate does not provide, and (c) the generalization across three registry classes with a head-to-head frontier that these works never run.

**Agent-mediated threats.** Recent work shows malicious persistent skills can steer benign coding agents toward attacker-controlled dependencies without touching weights or prompts, confirming that dependency selection is actively manipulable, not merely a passive model error. The OS-package-manager precedent — Sigstore, npm provenance attestations, PyPI trusted publishing — is our existence proof that the existence→signature→publisher-trust transition is deployable; agent-facing registries are simply stalled at existence while package managers have already moved.

## 3. Threat model and the attacker-satisfiability argument

**Setting.** Ecosystems $\mathcal{E}=\{\mathrm{npm},\mathrm{PyPI},\mathrm{MCP},\mathrm{skill}\}$. For each $e$, a public registry snapshot $\mathcal{R}_e$ carries install-time-observable metadata in schema $\mathcal{X}_e$. Published statistics $\theta$ describe hallucinated-name frequency and recurrence; $Q_\theta$ is the seed distribution for the simulated adversary; $H\subseteq[0,1]$ is the swept hallucination-rate range (4.6%–43%).

**Adversary.** For each $\rho\in H$ the simulator emits install events $\mathcal{D}_\rho=\mathcal{A}_\rho\cup\mathcal{L}_\rho$: adversarial installs $\mathcal{A}_\rho$ induced by hallucinated names drawn from $Q_\theta$, and legitimate installs $\mathcal{L}_\rho$ of real records. The adversary observes a predicted name at time $t_0$, chooses a **registration schedule** $\gamma\in\Gamma_i$ for each attack event $i$, and the schedule determines the install-time metadata $x_i^\gamma$ — existence, accrued age, seeded popularity. Attributes not obtainable by registration timing alone (e.g., a signature under a trusted publisher key) are held fixed by the adversary's actual capability. The attacker's power is exactly this: it controls **when** the clock starts, and the recurrence in $Q_\theta$ tells it which names are worth pre-registering.

**Predicate resistance.** An install-time predicate is $p:\mathcal{X}_e\to\{0,1\}$ ($1$=install, $0$=intercept). Event-level *timing resistance* is
$$u_p(i)=1-\max_{\gamma\in\Gamma_i}p(x_i^\gamma),$$
i.e. $u_p(i)=1$ iff **no** admissible schedule makes $p$ accept attack $i$. The empirical rate at $\rho$ is $U_p(\rho)=|\mathcal{A}_\rho|^{-1}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{A}_\rho}u_p(i)$. Existence, age, and popularity are **negative controls** $\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{neg}}$: the simulator lets a sufficiently early schedule satisfy them, so $u_p\equiv 0$ by construction — they are excluded from any timing-resistance claim. The trust-tiered provenance predicate is a *candidate*, evaluated by the same definition without assuming it is resistant; the paper's finding is where on the $U_p$ axis it lands, not an axiom that it is 1.

**The registration-cost table (headline artifact).** For each existence predicate class we tabulate, from real registry mechanics, the attacker cost to satisfy it: registration fee, lead time to accrue (age ≥ N days), effort/cost to seed (downloads ≥ K), and whether the recurrence distribution makes it *pre-registerable*. Graded predicates (age ≥ 90 days, downloads ≥ 10k) impose *real, low* cost, not zero cost; the table reports that cost honestly. If some composed graded predicate is genuinely expensive, it is reported as a partial existence-side defense (falsification condition F1).

**When timing becomes acquisition.** For a history-dependent gate and short lead time $L$, no fresh registration matures in $L$, so the only route is acquiring an already-qualified lineage — an aged account, a namespace with maintainer history. This route costs cash and consumes scarce transferable-identity capacity. We do **not** rely on a (nonexistent) marketplace dataset: we sweep an assumed per-transfer price $\rho_{\mathrm{acq}}$ over a declared monotone ladder and report the **flip price** $p^*(g)$ — the largest transfer price at which the gate is still reachable within budget. Provenance's flip price is qualitatively different: there is no market price for "a signature under a trusted publisher's key" short of compromising that publisher.

## 4. The trust-tiered provenance gate

The gate assigns each install request to one of four tiers by the strongest provenance evidence present at install time:

1. **Untrusted** — name resolves, no signature. (Existence-class; attacker-satisfiable.)
2. **Signed** — artifact carries a valid signature, but the signing identity is self-asserted. Raises the bar to "hold a key," which an attacker can self-generate — so this tier alone is *not* sufficient, and we measure that.
3. **Attested-publisher** — signature chains to an identity attested by a trusted authority (Sigstore keyless identity, npm provenance attestation, PyPI trusted publishing). The attacker cannot satisfy this without compromising a trusted identity provider or a real publisher's CI.
4. **Reputation-established** — attested publisher with an accrued, non-transferable reputation history.

The install-time decision admits at or above a configured tier. The security value of each tier is the attacker cost to satisfy it (identity compromise, not registration timing); the usability cost is the **legitimate-publisher friction** — the false-block rate on genuine installs and, crucially, the **cold-start penalty** for a legitimate new publisher who has not yet accrued attestation or reputation. The gate is not magic: §7 measures these frictions and scopes the security claim to "raises the bar from free to identity-compromise," never "unbreakable."

## 5. Experimental setup

**Simulator (C1).** An event-level simulator, seeded per §3. Three substrates: (i) npm + PyPI from public metadata dumps (names, ages, download counts, version history); (ii) MCP servers from public registry listings; (iii) an agent-skill marketplace schema from public skill-registry listings. The adversary is seeded from 2607.12340's recurrence structure to the extent published; because only aggregate rates may be public, **attacker prediction accuracy is an explicit swept axis in $[0,1]$**, and we report the frontier as a function of it — the position holds as long as prediction accuracy is non-negligible, which the mere existence of recurrence establishes. One simulator parameter (median package age / download distribution) is validated against real registry data to show the substrate matches reality.

**Defenses, all re-implemented in-simulator at published strength.**
1. Existence check (name resolves) — negative control.
2. RAG-grounding (name present in a real index) — re-implemented and **validated** against the published 40.8%→3.2% block / ~16% recall point; reproducing that point validates the simulator, and any divergence is itself reported.
3. Self-audit (LLM re-checks its recommendation) — at the published 51–53% accuracy.
4. Name-reservation (defender pre-reserves predicted names) — a race against the attacker, driven only by an information-separated defender candidate list; we deliberately keep this baseline *lightweight and clearly labeled evidence-limited*, since no independent forecast stream exists, and we report its result as a race outcome rather than a tuned system.
5. **Trust-tiered provenance gate** (§4).
6. *Boundary comparator (labeled non-registry-native):* a recommendation-bound authorization envelope (provider-signed envelope + witnessed publisher commitment), included only to mark whether effective protection requires information absent from native registry metadata — never counted as a registry-native defense.

**Measurement (C2–C4).** For each defense × substrate × rate: **security** = fraction of malicious pre-registered installs blocked (attack interception); **usability** = legitimate-install recall loss + provenance cold-start penalty; the **predicate-cost table**; the hallucination-rate sweep 4.6%–43%. Published numbers appear only as annotations; every plotted competitor is measured on the *same* simulated attack, on the same install-opportunity denominator.

**Falsification conditions (printed).**
- **F1.** If a composed graded-existence predicate (strict age + real-download + not-flagged) blocks the seeded attacker at low usability cost comparable to provenance, "existence fails by construction" is materially weakened — an important negative, reported.
- **F2.** If the provenance gate's cold-start friction dominates its security gain, provenance is not a usable improvement; the frontier claim fails.
- **F3.** If the result flips at the low-rate end (4.6%) — attacker yield collapses enough that existence checks suffice — the urgency and rate-robustness claims weaken.
- **F4.** If real registry metadata shows median age/download bars an attacker cannot clear within realistic lead time, the pre-registration attack is impractical and SC2 softens.

## 6. Research questions and expected results

We reduce the evaluation to four questions, each an implementable module producing a JSON verdict; block-bootstrap CIs use 10,000 resamples, master seed `20260813`.

- **RQ1 (predicate satisfiability & cost).** For each existence predicate class, what is the attacker cost and lead time to satisfy it, and the timing-resistance rate $U_p(\rho)$? *Expected:* $U_p\approx 0$ for existence/age/popularity across the sweep; the cost table shows each satisfiable within realistic lead time. Adjudicates the "by construction" claim and F1/F4.
- **RQ2 (security/usability frontier).** Per substrate and rate, does the trust-tiered provenance gate dominate the existence-class cluster on interception vs recall loss? *Expected:* the attested-publisher and reputation tiers dominate; the signed tier alone does not (quantifying why self-asserted signatures are insufficient). RAG-grounding reproduces near its published point, validating the simulator.
- **RQ3 (rate robustness).** Does the ordering survive the 4.6%–43% sweep and the attacker-prediction-accuracy sweep? *Expected:* the *structure* (existence fails in kind) is rate-robust; only attacker yield scales with rate. Adjudicates F3.
- **RQ4 (provenance failure modes & cold start).** What is the legitimate-publisher cold-start block rate at each tier, and how do key-compromise / self-attestation / publisher-typosquatting degrade the gate? *Expected:* measurable cold-start friction that the reputation tier trades against security; the claim is scoped to "free → identity-compromise." Adjudicates F2.

The lineage-acquisition route (§3) enters RQ1/RQ2 only as a **price-swept sensitivity** with interval prices and a reported flip price $p^*(g)$, never as a claim resting on marketplace data. MCP and skill substrates report per-class operating points with explicit sparsity caveats; where identifiable coverage is thin, results are reported as named scenarios rather than pooled into an unconditional claim.

## 7. Discussion and threats to validity

**Provenance failure modes.** Compromised signing keys, self-attested "publishers," typosquatted publisher identities, and cold-start for legitimate new publishers are real; §4/RQ4 measure the cold-start cost and we scope the security claim accordingly. Provenance moves the attacker's problem from *free registration timing* to *identity compromise* — a qualitatively higher bar the recurrence distribution does not help with — not to impossibility.

**External validity of the simulation.** The adversary is seeded from published recurrence, baselines are set at their published best, and prediction accuracy is a reported axis, so "provenance wins" is measured against a strong, literature-anchored attacker rather than a strawman. The RAG-grounding re-implementation's agreement with its published operating point is an explicit simulator-validity check. The lineage-acquisition price sweep replaces the (nonexistent) marketplace dataset with a reported flip price, so no conclusion rests on unavailable data.

**Per-class caveats.** Packages have rich download/version history; MCP servers and skills are newer and sparser. The generalization claim is that the existence-fails / provenance-holds *structure* recurs, not that one threshold transfers; we report per-class operating points.

**Deployment path.** For operators: the OS-package-manager transition (Sigstore, npm provenance, PyPI trusted publishing) is the template; agent-facing registries can adopt attested-publisher gating with a graded cold-start allowance, using our frontier to choose the tier.

## 8. Conclusion

Existence, age, and popularity are properties an adversary who predicts a recurrent hallucinated name can make true on their own schedule; grounding a name the attacker registered does not help. The trust decision must move from *existence* to *provenance* — a predicate the attacker cannot satisfy without compromising a trusted identity — across package indexes, MCP registries, and skill marketplaces alike. We make the argument measurable: a literature-seeded simulator, a registration-cost table proving existence predicates are cheaply satisfiable, and an operating-point frontier on which a trust-tiered provenance gate dominates the existence-class cluster, robust across the published hallucination-rate range. Registries must gate on provenance, not existence.
